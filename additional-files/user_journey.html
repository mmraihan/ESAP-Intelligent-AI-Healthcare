Value of Information (VOI) framework: for each of the top-150 lab tests, simulate "what if this result were available?" by sampling from the empirical distribution of that test in similar patients. Measure the drop in differential entropy. Rank tests by expected information gain.

The honest ceiling
With intake-only inputs — no post-admission data, no test results — the expected Recall@20 is 0.45–0.55. This is not a limitation of the model; it is the information-theoretic limit of what can be known before the patient is examined. The system's value is getting the doctor to a confident decision faster by surfacing what the data already knows before they walk in.

M-01
Differential Diagnosis
debate: 4/5 change
Changed
▾
Chosen approach
Tower A dual-head: saga/elasticnet LR (sparse block) + HistGradientBoosting (dense labs block) · Clinical-Longformer (Tower B) · CEHR-BERT (Tower C) · LR fusion + per-label isotonic calibration
Why thisA single linear model can't parallelize across 502 labels, produces dense coefficients that aren't clinically explainable, and can't be calibrated as-is. A split head — sparse LR for prior codes plus gradient boosting for the 87.6%-missing labs — with CEHR-BERT (which encodes visit timing that Med-BERT cannot) gives calibrated, explainable, higher-accuracy predictions. ▾ open for full reasoning + other models in the market
Why we chose this
Four compounding defects: (1) liblinear is strictly serial across 502 labels — slow and unparallelizable; (2) L2 penalty gives dense coefficient vectors, so "feature-level explanations" were not clinically defensible; (3) no calibration wrapper existed anywhere, violating the calibration requirement from day one; (4) median imputation on labs that are 87.6% missing destroys nearly all lab signal. The split head fixes missingness — sparse CCSR+meds go to elasticnet LR (real variable selection → honest sparse explanations), dense labs go to HistGradientBoosting whose native NaN-split treats "no prior creatinine" as a distinct state, not a fake median. Med-BERT → CEHR-BERT because CEHR-BERT's temporal gap tokens natively encode inter-visit timing (CHF 6 months ago ≠ 6 years ago) — Med-BERT cannot recover this without re-pretraining.
Clinical reasoning
A shown score of 0.85 for heart failure will now mean ~85% of similar patients actually have it — not an arbitrary logit. For the ~55% arriving with no prior visits (often the sickest), the system falls back to a calibrated age×sex population prior instead of degraded scores from empty inputs. The non-linear head learns real joint patterns (a 72yo on furosemide + metoprolol → elevated joint HF/HTN/diabetes probability).
Technical reasoning
Two structurally different feature blocks need different inductive biases: sparse multi-hot (~1,500 cols, mostly zero) → saga+elasticnet for correlated-feature selection + sparse coefficients; dense labs (~158 cols, 87.6% NaN) → HGB native missing-direction splits. Fusion is constrained to LR to keep per-label tower-weight coefficients auditable for the fairness mandate.
Other models in the market — is there anything better?
Purushotham 2018 shows GBM-class methods beat sparse LR by +5–8 macro-AUROC on MIMIC tabular features. CEHR-BERT (Pang 2021) was built for OMOP prior-admission timelines and beats Med-BERT on sparse-history patients — exactly the cold-start stratum. Clinical-Longformer (Li 2022) gives +3–5 AUROC over Bio_ClinicalBERT and its 4096-token window fits the median ~1020-token intake concatenation (Bio_ClinicalBERT's 512 cap would truncate half of admissions). THCM-CAL Recall@20 = 0.46 is the go/no-go anchor — any pooled intake-only Recall@20 > 0.60 is a leakage alarm.
Trade-offs
Clinical-Longformer now requires offline embedding caching (ml.feat_note_emb) — it cannot co-reside in VRAM with Qwen without OOM risk. The dual head doubles training bookkeeping and the sparse matrix must never be .toarray()'d (3.28 GB → crash). LR fusion sacrifices a little stacking performance for auditability.
Cold-start (~55% of patients)
Tower C masked out via has_history flag; Tower A runs on demographics+vitals only; an age-decile×gender label-prevalence prior from ml.labels becomes the primary ranking signal. Expected Recall@20 ≈ 0.28–0.34, reported separately — never pooled away.
Calibration
Per-label isotonic on a dedicated calibration slice carved from train (not val, not test). Tower B/C use single-scalar temperature scaling. Flag any label with ECE > 0.05, and any subgroup diverging > 0.03 from pooled ECE.
Implementation tip
X_sparse = hstack([X_prior_ccsr, X_meds]) → OneVsRestClassifier(LogisticRegression(solver='saga', penalty='elasticnet', l1_ratio=0.5, C=0.1, class_weight='balanced'), n_jobs=-1). Separately X_dense with NaN preserved → MultiOutputClassifier(HistGradientBoostingClassifier(...)). Concatenate the two probability outputs → fusion. Never .toarray() the combined sparse matrix.

debate: 3/2 keep
Kept + fixed
▾
Chosen approach
HistGradientBoostingClassifier + sample_weight imbalance correction + isotonic calibration (prefit on val) + Charlson Comorbidity Index feature
Why thisGradient boosting is the strongest family for tabular mortality — AUROC 0.87–0.91 vs 0.82–0.84 for linear models — and handles the 87.6%-missing labs natively without imputation. It needed three fixes to be production-safe: real imbalance correction, probability calibration, and the Charlson comorbidity score. ▾ open for full reasoning + other models in the market
Why we chose this
The algorithm family is right — but three fixes are mandatory before it can drive triage escalation. (1) class_weight is silently ignored by HistGradientBoostingClassifier — you must pass explicit sample_weight, or the 94.4% majority class collapses positive-class recall. (2) Isotonic calibration is required before the 0.05/0.10/0.25 risk-bucket thresholds mean anything. (3) The Charlson Comorbidity Index adds ~0.02–0.03 AUROC and anchors cold-start patients on 40 years of validated mortality evidence.
Clinical reasoning
When the line between a 4.9% and 5.1% score decides whether a patient gets a medium-risk flag, the numbers must map to real observed mortality, not just rank order. HGB's native missing handling matters: a patient with no prior creatinine is clinically different from one whose creatinine was 4.2 — and the model preserves that.
Technical reasoning
HGB is the only sklearn tree ensemble with native per-split NaN routing — decisive with 87.6% lab missingness. On MIMIC-IV mortality it hits AUROC 0.87–0.91 vs LR's 0.82–0.84, clearing the 0.85 bar without libomp or GPU. Isotonic > Platt: Platt's 2-param sigmoid underfits GBM's right tail exactly where the High/Critical thresholds live.
Other models in the market — is there anything better?
Tabular GBM on MIMIC-IV mortality consistently lands AUROC 0.87–0.91 (Sheikhalishahi 2020; Harutyunyan 2019 LR floor 0.845). Transformer models (BEHRT 0.87, Hi-BEHRT 0.90–0.93) only win on tokenized timelines — those are Tower C, not this tabular head. Clinical scores (NEWS2 0.83–0.87, APACHE II 0.83–0.88) approach but don't reliably clear 0.85 and need ICU inputs unavailable at t0. No tabular-only CPU model materially beats tuned GBM; pooled AUROC > 0.93 = leakage alarm.
Trade-offs
Gives up LR's coefficient-level auditability (mitigated by SHAP + monotonic constraints) and pre-calibration probability quality. A stacked dense/sparse two-path ensemble could add another 0.02–0.04 AUROC but its cross-fitting complexity is premature before the feature set stabilizes — revisit in Phase 2.
Cold-start
Charlson=0, no prior features → model becomes an age-vitals-acuity submodel. Not extrapolation: ~55% of the training cohort follows this path, so leaf estimates there are stable. A healthy 25yo correctly gets a low score on demographics alone.
Calibration
Fit on 70% train with sample_weight, then CalibratedClassifierCV(cv='prefit', method='isotonic') on the 15% val split only. Target ECE < 0.03; reliability diagrams stratified by age decile, admission type, anchor_year_group.
Implementation tip
sw = np.where(y_train==1, n_neg/n_pos, 1.0); hgb.fit(X, y, sample_weight=sw) — never rely on the constructor's class_weight (silently ignored). Then cal = CalibratedClassifierCV(hgb, cv='prefit', method='isotonic'); cal.fit(X_val, y_val). Serialize cal, not hgb. Apply thresholds to cal.predict_proba() only.

M-03
ICU Admission Prediction
debate: 3/2 keep
Kept + fixed
▾
Chosen approach
HistGradientBoostingClassifier + sample_weight balanced + isotonic calibration (prefit on val) + monotonic constraints on age/Charlson/n_prior
Why thisSame as mortality: gradient boosting leads tabular ICU prediction (AUROC 0.83–0.88) and routes missing values natively — critical when ~55% of patients arrive with no history. It needed calibration and imbalance correction before it can safely drive bed-management routing. ▾ open for full reasoning + other models in the market
Why we chose this
Base algorithm unchanged. Three fixes before it connects to bed-management routing: (1) replace the unsupported class_weight with compute_sample_weight('balanced', y_train); (2) wrap in isotonic CalibratedClassifierCV(cv='prefit') on the val split — not cv=5, which would refit five full models on 546k rows; (3) densify the sparse matrix in batches (~2.4 GB budget, float16 safe). Also remove lightgbm from requirements.txt — it's uninstallable on this host.
Clinical reasoning
Bed management pre-requests ICU beds off these probabilities — so they must be accurate in absolute terms, not just ranked. A patient scored 38% whose true risk is 43% gets routed to step-down instead of ICU: a patient-safety event. Isotonic calibration makes the 15%/40% routing thresholds clinically meaningful.
Technical reasoning
At t0, ~55% have all CCSR/med columns as NaN (not zero) and 88% have missing labs — HGB's native NaN routing avoids injecting imputation noise at peak uncertainty. cv='prefit' isotonic on the pre-built val split cuts ECE from 0.04–0.09 to <0.025 at both thresholds without the 5× refit cost.
Other models in the market — is there anything better?
GBTs are best-in-class for tabular ICU prediction on MIMIC: XGBoost/LightGBM/CatBoost report AUROC 0.83–0.88 (Mao 2018; Grinsztajn NeurIPS 2022), above the 0.80 target. XGBoost pip-installs without libomp and is the recommended follow-on if pooled AUROC doesn't clear 0.82. Transformers need sequences/notes absent at t0 (Tower B/C, not here). NEWS2 (0.74–0.79) is below target and needs RR/SpO2 often missing at t0.
Trade-offs
HGB over sparse LR: +3–5 AUROC but loses CSR sparse compatibility (one-time ~2.4 GB dense conversion at train; serving is microseconds). cv='prefit' saves 4–6 hrs CPU vs cv=5. Pooled AUROC ~0.83 masks a cold-start subgroup ~0.70–0.75 — the age-sex prevalence fallback must ship before production.
Cold-start (~55%)
HGB routes NaN through learned child directions; subgroup AUROC ~0.70–0.75 driven by the 13 dense columns. Must be reported separately — pooled 0.83 hiding cold-start 0.72 is not acceptable for 24/7 bed routing. Surface a CI, not a hard decision, in the 12–18% uncertainty band.
Calibration
Isotonic (not Platt) on the 15% val split. Verify ECE stratified at both routing thresholds and by arrived_via_ed / age group / anchor_year_group / cold-start flag. Block deploy if any stratum ECE > 0.03. Expect ~60% ward / 25% step-down / 15% ICU.
Implementation tip
sw = compute_sample_weight('balanced', y_train); base.fit(X, y, sample_weight=sw) → CalibratedClassifierCV(base, method='isotonic', cv='prefit').fit(X_val, y_val). Add monotone_cst={age:1, charlson:1, n_prior:1} to prevent SHAP-confusing reversals on features clinicians expect to only increase risk.

M-04
30-Day Readmission Risk
debate: 4/5 change
Changed
▾
Chosen approach
HistGradientBoostingClassifier + monotonic_cst on polypharmacy/Charlson/prior-admits + isotonic calibration + SHAP top-3 driver statements · LACE index as cold-start floor feature
Why thisA linear model treats going from 4 to 5 medications the same as 14 to 15 — missing the polypharmacy threshold that matters most for readmission. Gradient boosting learns that threshold (+5–8 AUROC) and, paired with SHAP, still hands social workers plain-English reasons for every flag. ▾ open for full reasoning + other models in the market
Why we chose this
Vanilla LR fails in three decisive ways here: (1) it assigns a constant linear slope to polypharmacy — the explicitly key feature — missing the well-documented threshold effect above 5–10 concurrent meds; (2) it imputes missing vitals/labs as median-normal, mislabeling the highest-risk emergency admissions who have no pre-admission workup; (3) its raw probabilities have ECE 4–9% in the upper tail, making the 18%/30% social-work thresholds unreliable. HGB fixes all three with no new dependency, and — crucially — the interpretability requirement is satisfied by SHAP plain-English driver statements ("11 active meds, 2 prior ED visits, active heart failure"), which is what social workers actually see, not LR coefficients they never look at.
Clinical reasoning
A patient on 12+ meds carries categorically higher risk than one on 4, but LR treats drug 4→5 the same as 14→15 — under-scoring exactly the complex patients who most need social-work engagement. HGB learns the threshold directly, and every alert carries a ranked plain-English "why". Isotonic calibration makes a 22% score mean roughly 1-in-5 readmit.
Technical reasoning
HGB handles NaN natively, avoiding the median-imputation artifact that maps missing-lab (disproportionately urgent) patients to spuriously normal values. On 15% positive rate at 546k rows the AUROC gap vs LR is +5–8 pts (0.75–0.78 vs 0.68–0.72). monotonic_cst (sklearn 1.6.1) forces polypharmacy/Charlson/prior-admits to only increase risk, preventing counterintuitive SHAP attributions.
Other models in the market — is there anything better?
Honest intake-only ceiling on MIMIC-IV is AUROC 0.75–0.78 with GBTs (Rajkomar 2018) vs 0.68–0.72 for calibrated LR (Shameer 2017, Futoma 2015). HOSPITAL Score (0.72) and LACE Index (0.68) are computable from the existing schema and kept as audit comparators + cold-start fallbacks. All transformers (ClinicalBERT, BEHRT, NYUTron…) are infeasible: they need post-discharge note content that violates the t0 leakage rule. NYUTron's 0.83 uses full discharge summaries; with admission-only sections it drops to ~0.73 — within HGB's reach on structured data without the leakage risk.
Trade-offs
Loses full coefficient legibility (mitigated by SHAP + monotonic constraints) and true online partial_fit (mitigated by a 4-hour background retrain). If a future compliance rule demands coefficient transparency, migrate to SGDClassifier(loss='log_loss', penalty='elasticnet') — but it shares LR's polypharmacy linearity flaw, so not as primary.
Cold-start (~55%)
Falls back to dense features (age, sex, BMI, BP, polypharmacy, admission type). class_weight='balanced' stops majority-class default. Inject LACE index (all 4 components derivable from the existing ml schema, no new ETL) as a dense feature anchoring cold-start to a validated baseline.
Calibration
Isotonic CalibratedClassifierCV(cv=5). Stratified reliability diagrams for has_note / has_prior_history / anchor_year_group / admission_type. Gate deploy on ECE < 0.05 at each tier (8/18/30%). Very-High precision must be ≥ 0.40 before enabling auto-trigger.
Implementation tip
monotonic_cst={'polypharmacy_count':1, 'charlson_score':1, 'n_prior_admissions_90d':1}. For SHAP, reach the base via calibrated_model.calibrated_classifiers_[0].estimator → shap.TreeExplainer. Map internal names to plain English so alerts read as natural-language bullets without exposing model internals.

M-05
Length of Stay Prediction
debate: 3/5 change (family unanimous)
Changed
▾
Chosen approach
3-model HistGradientBoostingRegressor quantile ensemble (loss='quantile' @ 0.025/0.5/0.975) on log1p(LOS) + split-conformal calibration for a distribution-free 95% CI
Why thisA single predicted day with no confidence interval is a hard blocker for bed planning — a coordinator needs a range, not a point. A three-model quantile ensemble gives a patient-specific 95% interval with a distribution-free coverage guarantee, using the same algorithm family and no new dependencies. ▾ open for full reasoning + other models in the market
Why we chose this
The single squared_error model produces no confidence interval — a hard deployment blocker, since the clinical requirement is a 95% CI for bed management. The quantile ensemble resolves it with zero new dependencies. The algorithm family (HistGradientBoostingRegressor) is kept — what changes is the loss function, one model → three, and added conformal calibration. Key math: quantile functions are equivariant under monotone transforms, so fitting on log1p(LOS) and back-transforming via expm1 gives correct quantiles on the raw scale. Jensen's inequality bias only hits the mean estimator, not quantile estimators — so the log transform is safe and numerically beneficial.
Clinical reasoning
A single predicted day without a range isn't actionable — a coordinator needs to know if "Day 5" means "almost certainly Day 5" or "anywhere from Day 3 to Day 12." The 95% interval is asymmetric and patient-specific: a tight 2-day band for an elective knee replacement, a wide one for a septic multi-organ patient. The point estimate is the median — better for scheduling than a mean inflated by rare 100-day outliers.
Technical reasoning
MIMIC-IV LOS is right-skewed with a heavy tail; squared_error on log-LOS estimates the geometric mean, underestimating arithmetic-mean LOS for complex patients (Jensen). Quantile regression is immune. Three independent HGB fits minimize separate pinball losses; split-conformal on the ~82k-row val split then gives a distribution-free finite-sample 95% coverage guarantee — no distributional assumptions.
Other models in the market — is there anything better?
Published MIMIC-IV GBM tabular floors (Turgeman 2017, PyHealth 2023) sit at MAE ~2.75–3.12 days, R² ~0.27–0.31 — right for Tower A's cold-start-fallback role. Tower B (Clinical-Longformer, RMSE ~0.41–0.49 log-days) and Tower C (CEHR-BERT, ~0.44) add lift for note-bearing / repeat patients but need GPU and don't change the tabular anchor. MAPIE was considered and rejected — split-conformal from val residuals gives the same guarantee without the dependency. Poisson loss is valid for the mean, but the clinical use case wants the median, which quantile regression delivers directly.
Trade-offs
3× training time (~30–90 min on 4 cores — acceptable per project constraints). Point estimate is the median not the mean (document this if any capacity system expects arithmetic mean). Quantile crossing must be guarded with clip. Conformal gives marginal not conditional coverage — stratified passes needed if any subgroup drops below 92%. No aleatoric/epistemic uncertainty decomposition.
Cold-start (~55%)
Receives has_prior_history=False, n_prior_admissions=0 as explicit features, so the q=0.025/0.975 models learn wider intervals — the model honestly signals its own uncertainty. Tower B (notes) is the primary signal carrier for cold-start; Tower A median is the fallback.
Calibration (conformal, not isotonic)
Split-conformal in log-space on the val split: δ = quantile(residuals, 0.95·(1+1/n)); serving interval [expm1(q025−δ), expm1(q975+δ)]. Verify coverage per LOS decile; stratum-specific δ if any >14-day decile drops below 90%.
Implementation tip
Wrap each quantile model in TransformedTargetRegressor(regressor=HistGradientBoostingRegressor(loss='quantile', quantile=q, ...), func=np.log1p, inverse_func=np.expm1). Tune max_iter via early stopping on the q=0.5 model, then fix it for q=0.025/0.975. Serialize as one dict {'point', 'lower', 'upper', 'conformal_delta'}. Guard crossing: ci_hi = max(upper, point). Feed M-01's top-10 CCSR probabilities (not the argmax) as a dense block to preserve uncertainty.

Step 1 of 6

The Big Picture

It reads everything known about a patient the moment they arrive and hands the doctor a fast, ranked shortlist of what's most likely going on — a helper, never a verdict.

Picture a patient walking into the emergency room (the ER) with chest pain and shortness of breath. The nurse jots down their age, takes their blood pressure, asks what medicines they take at home, and pulls up their old records. Now a doctor has to look at all of that and think, "Okay, what could be wrong here?" That mental shortlist of likely suspects — ranked from most to least likely — is called a differential diagnosis. It's not a final answer. It's the top candidates worth investigating.

Building that list is slow work. A doctor can spend 15 to 20 minutes reading notes before they even walk into the room, and tired humans sometimes miss a detail buried in a long chart. So we're building a computer assistant that does a fast first pass — in seconds. The key thing to hold onto: it's a shortlist for the doctor, not a verdict. It never says "you have disease X." It says "here are the conditions this patient most likely already has — usually the doctor eyes the top 10 or so — ranked, each with a confidence score and a short reason why."

Under the hood, it's really three mini "specialists," each fluent in a different shape of information: one reads tidy numbers (a neat table of measurements like blood pressure and lab values), one reads the free-written words of the intake notes, and one reads the patient's past visits as a time-ordered timeline — what was diagnosed, and in what order, across years. Same patient, but very different formats, which is why each specialist has to be trained separately. A small "referee" then combines their three opinions into one list — and it has learned from thousands of past cases whose opinion to trust more in which situation, for example leaning on the note-reader when there's a rich write-up and on the timeline-reader for a patient with a long history. But the big picture is simpler than that: fast, ranked, explained, and always leaving the final call to the doctor.

It hands over a shortlist, not a verdict
The output is a ranked list — highest confidence at the top — of the most likely conditions, each with a short 'why' drawn straight from the patient's own data (for example: 'flagged heart failure partly because of the leg swelling noted at intake and a heart attack three years ago'). Think suspects, not a conviction. The doctor still examines the patient and orders the tests — the tool just narrows the field fast so nothing obvious slips through.
It can flag several problems at once
Real patients often have more than one thing going on — say diabetes AND high blood pressure AND heart failure all together. The tool is 'multi-label,' which just means it can tick many boxes instead of being forced to pick one answer, like a form where you check everything that applies.
It only sees what's known the moment they arrive
Age, vitals like blood pressure, diagnoses from past visits, home medicines, any early lab results, and the intake notes about why they came — that's all it gets. It's blocked from peeking at anything that happens later in the stay. Here's what that peeking would look like in practice: the discharge summary, written when the patient leaves, usually names the final diagnosis — if that text slipped into the inputs, the tool would basically be reading the answer key instead of predicting. That accidental peeking is called 'leakage,' and it's guarded against very carefully. As genuinely new information arrives during the stay — fresh labs, a new note — the tool re-runs on its own and sharpens its list.
It's honest about how sure it is — and about its limits
A confidence score is the tool's estimate of the chance the patient truly has that condition right now — so '80%' means 'about an 80% chance this one is present,' not that it's the single right answer. For those numbers to be trustworthy they're 'calibrated': you take every case where it said around 80%, then check that roughly 80% of those patients really had the condition; if only 60% did, it was overconfident, so the numbers get dialled down to match reality. It won't catch everything — a realistic goal is that about half the time, the patient's real condition lands somewhere in the top 20 guesses (we give the test a little more room than a doctor's quick glance — hence 20, not 10). The rest of the time it's usually partly right, not blank. And for a very rare condition it has barely seen, it's weaker and leans on general age-and-sex odds — one more reason the doctor always stays in the loop.
Think of it like…
It's like your phone's map app guessing where you're headed and how long it'll take the moment you start the car — and it does that not from fixed rules, but from patterns it has learned across millions of past trips. It's fast, it narrows your options, and it's usually right — but you're still the one driving, and you decide which way to actually go.

Step 2 of 6

What Goes In, What Comes Out

The model reads a patient's check-in paperwork and hands the doctor a ranked shortlist of the conditions they most likely already have.

Picture an experienced ER nurse. A patient walks in, the nurse glances at their intake paperwork, and within seconds says: "Here are my top guesses for what's going on with this person, and here's why." That's basically what this model does. And by "model" we just mean the computer program itself — a math system that taught itself patterns by studying huge piles of past patient records. It uses math where the nurse uses instinct.

So the real question is simple: what does it get to look at, and what does it hand back? What goes IN is a snapshot of the patient frozen at one single moment — the second they're checked in. What comes OUT is a ranked shortlist of the conditions (that is, the diseases or diagnoses) they most likely already have, each with a confidence score and a short reason.

One rule sits underneath all of it: the model is only allowed to see what's known at check-in. Nothing that happens later in the hospital stay is permitted. That single rule shapes everything else — including why the model is never expected to be perfect.

What goes in: a single-moment snapshot
Think of a form the hospital fills out the instant a patient is admitted. It holds basic facts (age, sex, how they arrived), recent body measurements like blood pressure and BMI (Body Mass Index — a single number from a person's height and weight that flags whether they're under- or overweight), any conditions recorded during past hospital visits, the medicines they were already taking at home, any early blood-test results, and the 'safe' parts of the doctor's admission note. By 'safe' we mean the parts written at check-in that describe why the patient came — the complaint, relevant old history — but NOT anything added later, like the final diagnosis. Those later parts are excluded on purpose, because they'd hand the model the very answer it's supposed to work out. Everything here is captured at one moment: check-in. Anything that happens later in the stay is off-limits.
What comes out: a ranked shortlist, not a verdict
The model doesn't give one answer. Behind the scenes it quietly scores every condition it knows about — about 500 of them, drawn from a standard medical catalog that sorts every diagnosis into roughly 500 groups — and ranks them most-to-least likely, highest confidence on top, like any ranked list. It's 'multi-label,' which just means more than one thing can be true at once — like a package that can wear both a 'fragile' sticker AND a 'this side up' sticker — so it can flag diabetes AND high blood pressure AND heart failure together. Each guess carries a confidence number and a short 'why' (which inputs pushed it up). And here's a common misread: the list isn't a forced length. Only the conditions that score high enough to matter actually surface — the other hundreds sink toward zero and aren't shown — so a healthy person might show 2 or 3 real guesses while a complicated patient shows 15. The doctor still decides, the way you still pick the road even after the GPS offers three routes.
Where the confidence number comes from, and what 'honest' means
First, on wording: 'confidence score,' 'confidence number,' and 'percentage' all mean the same single thing — one number per guess, like 75%. It's not a gut feeling with a number slapped on. It's calculated from thousands of past patients whose real diagnoses we already know. A '75%' roughly means: of all the past patients whose paperwork looked like this one's, about 75 out of 100 turned out to have diabetes — exactly like a forecaster's '70% chance of rain.' A model is 'honest' — the technical word is 'calibrated' — when its numbers mean what they claim: when it says 80%, it turns out right about 80% of the time. Can a computer lie about this? Not on purpose, but it can be wrong in a checkable way — like a forecaster who always cries '90% rain' when it only actually rains 60% of the time. The people who build the model check this before it's ever used on a patient: a computer gathers every case where the model said '75%,' counts how many actually turned out right, and plots claimed-versus-actual on a chart to see if the two line up. That's what 'checking in bulk' means — grading thousands of past predictions at once, after the real outcomes are known.
Why check-in only, and why catching half is a win
Imagine predicting tomorrow's rain while secretly peeking at tomorrow's weather report. You'd look brilliant and be useless — that report doesn't exist yet when the forecast is actually needed. Same here: if the model saw the treatments or the discharge summary that literally states the final diagnosis, it would look like a genius on paper and fail in real life. So it's tested the honest way, on only what's known at admission. To grade it fairly, researchers use one fixed yardstick: they take the model's 20 highest-scored conditions and count how many of the patient's true conditions (the diagnoses those patients were eventually confirmed to have, which the records tell us) landed in that top 20. That fixed 20 is just a measuring ruler — separate from the shorter, variable list a doctor actually reads. By this yardstick, a strong model catches only about half, and that's realistic, not a fail. Here's why half is good: with about 500 possible conditions, picking 20 at random gives any single true condition only a 20-in-500 shot — roughly 4 in 100 — of making the list, so landing near half is more than ten times better than blind guessing. And remember how little the model is working from: a doctor eventually has the whole hospital stay — days of monitoring, results, how the patient responds to treatment — while the model gets only that first check-in snapshot. The half it misses is exactly why doctors keep testing after admission; the model is a fast first-pass assistant, not a replacement.
Think of it like…
It's like an experienced ER nurse who glances at a patient's intake form the moment they walk in and rattles off a ranked 'top guesses' list, with a reason for each. The intake form is what goes in; the ranked list of likely conditions is what comes out. The nurse doesn't get to see how the hospital stay ends — and neither does the model.

Step 3 of 6

Why We Chose This Model

Different kinds of patient data need different tools, so instead of one do-everything program we use three specialist mini-models plus a combiner that blends their guesses and a final honesty check that keeps the percentages trustworthy.

When a patient walks into the ER, the information about them isn't one neat thing. It sits in three very different piles: a form full of checkboxes and numbers (age, blood pressure, past diseases, home medicines, labs), a paragraph of written notes from the nurse ("chest pain since this morning..."), and a timeline of past hospital visits, like a diary of what went wrong before.

Our job is to build a model — and by model we just mean a computer program that takes information in and hands a guess back out. Here's the whole idea in one line: those three piles don't look alike, so no single program reads all of them well. A program that's great with spreadsheets can't read a sentence; a program built to read sentences is put together the wrong way for a spreadsheet. (That "wrong shape" is a figure of speech — it means built for a different job, like a key cut for a different lock.)

So we didn't force one model to do everything. We built three specialists — we nickname them "towers" because each stands on its own as a self-contained mini-model, and we picture them lined up side by side. Each is expert at exactly one pile. Then a small "combiner" listens to all three and blends their opinions into the final answer a doctor sees: not one disease name, but a ranked list of possible conditions, each with a percentage beside it. A last step called "calibration" makes sure those percentages are honest. More moving parts than one simple model, yes — but that's what buys both accuracy and the trust a medical tool can't skip.

Three piles of data, so three specialists
Each tower is a mini-model tuned for one kind of data. Tower A handles the checkbox-and-number pile. It's fast, cheap, and see-through: a doctor can read exactly why it leaned a certain way. Where does a figure like '+20% toward heart failure' come from? During training it saw huge numbers of past patients and measured how much high blood pressure actually shifted the odds; it stored that as a weight and now applies it. Tower B handles the written notes. Tower C handles the timeline of past visits, working a bit like phone autocomplete — just as autocomplete learns which word tends to follow another, Tower C learned which conditions tend to follow a given history, and reads your past visits to predict what's likely present now. (Calling numbers or notes a 'language' is just shorthand for 'a kind of data with its own patterns.') Three specialists each fluent in one beat a single generalist doing a mediocre job on all three. Tower A is see-through by nature; B and C are harder to peek inside, which is exactly why the project adds a separate explanation layer to trace their reasoning — they're not left as black boxes.
What it means for Tower B to 'read' a note
Tower B turns each word into a list of numbers — picture coordinates that pin the word somewhere on a giant map, a few hundred numbers long. Words with similar meanings get pinned near each other ('chest pain' and 'cardiac pain' land close; 'chest pain' and 'banana' land far apart), and 'near' simply means their number-lists are alike. How did it learn where to place each word? Ahead of time it read millions of sentences, over and over covering up a word and trying to guess it from the ones around it; each time it checked its guess against the real word and nudged its numbers to do a little better. That guess-check-nudge, repeated endlessly, is all 'learning' means here. Because it reads words in order and looks at each word's neighbors, 'no chest pain' and 'chest pain' don't come out the same. It doesn't understand like a person — more like a chef who's tasted ten thousand dishes and can spot the cumin in a new one, purely from pattern.
The combiner weighs the opinions — and learns how
The combiner is itself a small mini-model — 'small' because it has so little to do: it never touches raw notes or labs, it only takes the three towers' guesses plus a few plain facts and blends them. Humans decide which facts it may notice (Does this patient have a note at all? How many past visits are on file?), but nobody hand-writes rules like 'trust Tower C 30% less.' Instead it practices on thousands of past patients whose true diagnoses we already know: it blends the towers' guesses, compares its blend to the known right answer, and nudges its internal dials so next time it lands closer — the same guess-check-nudge as before. Over thousands of rounds it works out on its own things like 'when there are only 2 past visits, Tower C is shaky — lean on it less.' Those known-answer patients are used only for this practice. With a brand-new patient it has never seen, no answer is handed over — it just applies what it learned and produces a fresh guess; it is not looking anything up. And if that new patient has no history, there's nothing for Tower C to read, so it leans on A and B.
Calibration makes '80%' actually mean 80%
Every model here outputs a dial between 0% and 100%, not just yes/no. That percentage falls out of the same math the model uses to decide: as it weighs the evidence, the strength of that evidence comes out as a number we read as a percent. But a percentage-shaped number isn't automatically an honest one, the way a bathroom scale can read 5 lbs high and still 'work.' So we test the model on old patients whose real diagnoses we recorded but kept hidden from it during training — an honest answer key. Take the thousand patients it called '80%.' If only 500 of them — that's half, or 50% — actually had the disease, then its '80%' really behaves like '50%.' Calibration is a small correction step that catches this and adjusts every future number to match — here it would pull that inflated 80% down toward 50%. ('Stretch or squish' just names that nudge: up when the model is under-confident, down when it's over-confident.) For a clinician betting a decision on the number, that honesty is required, not a nice-to-have.
Think of it like…
Think of a toolbox. A screwdriver is perfect for screws and useless for cutting wood; a saw is the reverse. You wouldn't grab only a hammer for every job in the house just because one tool is simpler — you'd do a bad job everywhere. Our three towers are the right tool for each pile of data, and the combiner is the head doctor who listens to all three specialists and weighs each opinion by how much evidence it actually had.

Step 4 of 6

What Else Could We Use

No single tool is great at everything, so instead of picking one all-purpose model, we split the job among specialists and let a small "referee" combine their answers.

Quick reminder of the job. When a patient arrives, we want a short, ranked list of the diseases they probably already have — drawn from the roughly 500 conditions we track (502, to be exact) — a computer-generated gut-check for the doctor, produced in seconds.

One word first, since we lean on it constantly: a *model* is just a program that has studied lots of past patient records — real cases where we already know which diseases the person turned out to have — and picked up on the patterns. "Training" a model means showing it those answered examples and letting it adjust itself until its guesses match the known answers; after that, it can take a brand-new patient and guess on its own. Different models learn in different ways, and that difference is the whole story here.

Each option is quietly juggling four things at once: accuracy (does it guess right?), honest confidence (when it says "70% sure," is it right about 70% of the time?), explainability (can we show the doctor *why*?), and compute cost — how much expensive computer processing, electricity, and time it eats. Some models run on a laptop in a blink; others need banks of specialized chips we don't have spare. No single tool wins all four, so let's try them out one at a time.

Simple models: honest but narrow
Logistic Regression works like a scorecard. Each clue about the patient ("has diabetes: yes," "blood pressure: 130") gets multiplied by a *weight* — a number that says how much that clue matters for a given disease. Where do the weights come from? Training: the model starts with rough guesses for them and nudges them, record by record, until its scores line up with the real answers. It then adds up the weighted clues and squashes the total into a number between 0 and 1 — that's the "confidence." You can check whether that confidence is honest after the fact: gather every patient it called "70% likely" and see if about 70% of them truly had the disease. Logistic Regression tends to pass. It's also easy to explain (just print the scorecard) — but it only understands *tidy* numbers: values already sorted into clean labeled slots, like a blood-pressure column or a yes/no box. It can't read a doctor's free-form note or a jumbled history of past visits.
Tree models: strong on tables, blind to the rest
A *decision tree* is a flowchart of yes/no questions that ends in a guess. Random Forest asks 500 different trees and goes with the majority vote. XGBoost is its sharper cousin: it builds trees one after another, and each new tree is trained to focus on the cases the earlier ones got wrong — that "fix the last mistake" habit is exactly how it learns. Both shine on messy *tables* — grids of numbers riddled with gaps, like lab results where most patients are missing most values. Because XGBoost's fix-the-mistakes style usually edges out the forest's plain vote on accuracy, when we needed one table-cruncher for the messy lab numbers we picked the sharper of the two — XGBoost — and used it for that job only. Both are still blind to free text and visit timelines.
One giant model or an LLM: powerful but hard to trust
You could instead train one giant deep-learning model on everything. Such a model passes the data through dozens of stacked *layers* — each layer takes the previous one's output, remixes it, and hands it on. That's what makes it a *black box*: by the final layer the original clues are blended beyond recognition, like a smoothie you can't un-blend back into separate fruits, so there's no clean scorecard to show. The best anyone can do is an approximate reconstruction of what mattered — and that after-the-fact reconstruction is the guess (our guess about the model's reasoning), not the model's actual reasoning. A *Large Language Model* (LLM) — a model trained on enormous amounts of written text — is wonderful at reading messy notes and writing plain-English sentences, but it can "hallucinate" (state something false as if it were fact), which is dangerous in medicine, and it can't reliably produce one honest 0-to-1 score for each of our ~500 conditions. Both also need far more compute than we have spare. So we don't hand the notes to the LLM; a purpose-built note-reader does that (next), and the LLM stays a small helper — for example, tidying a messy drug name into a standard one.
Our answer: hire specialists, add a referee
So we split the problem by the *kind* of data. The tidy yes/no facts — the patient's *history* (which conditions they were treated for in earlier hospital stays) and their *home meds* (the medicines they were already taking before arriving) — go to the calculator-style Logistic Regression. The messy lab numbers go to XGBoost. The doctor's written notes go to a *text-reader*: a deep-learning model built specifically to read clinical text (a cousin of the LLM, but one that outputs scores, not sentences). And the patient's *timeline* — the ordered list, visit by visit, of what happened in the past — goes to a *sequence-reader*, a model built to read events in order. Then a small *referee* combines their opinions. It isn't a plain average: the referee is trained the same way the others are — on past patients where each specialist's guess and the true answer are both known — so it learns whom to trust when, leaning on the text-reader when there's a good note and ignoring it when the chart is empty. That buys us most of a giant model's accuracy, but with honest confidence and a compute bill small enough to actually run.
Think of it like…
Think of a home renovation. You wouldn't hire one person to do the wiring, the plumbing, and the cabinets — you'd call the right specialist for each, then have a project manager pull all their work together into one finished kitchen. That's exactly what we did: a different mini-model for each kind of patient data, plus a small "referee" (the project manager) to combine their answers.

How We Know It Works

We grade the model with three different report cards, because being "good" means three different things — and it has to pass all three, not just one.

Picture our model as a fast, well-read intern. The moment a patient walks in, it reads their chart and hands the doctor a sticky note: a short, ranked shortlist of the conditions this person most likely already has, picked out of a fixed menu of about 502 possible disease categories. (We'll say conditions, diagnoses, and diseases interchangeably — here they all mean the same thing.) The doctor still makes every real decision; this is just a smart shortlist to point them in the right direction fast.

So how do we know the intern is any good? First, a fair test needs an answer key — so we grade it on past patients whose true conditions are already recorded in their files. Because we know what was really wrong with them, we can check the intern's shortlist against reality. Then we grade with three different report cards. That sounds like overkill, but "good" means three different things depending on what you're checking.

One report card asks whether the right answers made the list. One asks whether we're any good at the rare, dangerous stuff without just guessing everything. And one asks whether we can trust the confidence numbers the model shows. A model has to pass all three — acing just one isn't enough when a real doctor is leaning on the results.

Recall@10 — did the right answers show up?
Recall@10 reads out loud as 'recall at 10,' and it asks one simple thing. Say a patient really has 4 conditions and the model writes down its 10 best guesses: how many of those 4 real ones landed in the top 10? If 2 of 4 made it, that's 50 percent. We track it at two list lengths — Recall@10 (strict: did they make the top 10?) and Recall@20 (looser: the top 20?) — the same measurement, just how far down the list we're willing to look. This mirrors how a busy doctor actually uses the tool: they scan a short list, they don't read all 502 possibilities. One honest caveat: recall only checks whether the true answers showed up — it doesn't punish the model for padding the rest of the list with junk (that's the next report card's job). A realistic good score is 45 to 55 percent landing in the top 20. If a version suddenly claims 90 percent, that's a red flag: it usually means the model peeked at information it shouldn't have had yet. Concretely — imagine it's fed a blood-sugar test that doctors only order once they already suspect diabetes. Seeing that test, the model 'predicts' diabetes and looks brilliant, but at a real intake, before anyone suspected anything, that test wouldn't exist yet. It didn't predict the answer; it read it off a test that only happens after the diagnosis.
AUPRC — are we good at the rare stuff without crying wolf?
AUPRC stands for 'Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve' — a mouthful, so let's build it up. It balances two things. Precision: of everything I flagged, how much was actually right? (Am I crying wolf?) Recall: of everything that was truly there, how much did I catch? (Am I missing things?) That recall is the same idea as in the first report card — what fraction of the true conditions did we catch — but 'caught' now means something different. Before, 'caught' meant 'made the top 10.' Here, 'caught' means 'the model was sure enough to flag it.' The model quietly gives every one of the 502 conditions a confidence number, and a cutoff is the line where it decides 'only flag this if I'm at least this sure.' (This can disagree with a plain top 10: if the model is only 30 percent sure about all ten of its guesses, a high cutoff flags none of them.) Set the cutoff low and you catch almost everything but sound a lot of false alarms; set it high and you stay clean but miss things — like a smoke detector's sensitivity dial. AUPRC doesn't pick one setting. It slides the cutoff across every possible value; each setting gives one precision-and-recall pair — one dot — and all the dots together trace a curve. The area under that curve (a single score from 0 to 1, higher is better) is AUPRC. A model that stays accurate while still catching most real cases traces a curve that hugs the top, so the area is big. Because you need both precision and recall to be high, a lazy 'flag everything' model — which catches everything (great recall) but is wrong constantly (terrible precision) — scores badly. And here's why it shines on rare-but-serious conditions: if a disease shows up in only 1 patient out of 100, a model that always says 'nope, not present' is right 99 percent of the time — plain accuracy would call it excellent even though it never catches the disease. AUPRC ignores all those easy correct 'no's and looks only at how well you flag the real cases, so it can't be fooled that way. That's exactly the safety net a doctor needs most.
Calibration and ECE — does 80% actually mean 80%?
The model doesn't just rank conditions — it attaches that confidence number to each, like '80 percent chance of pneumonia.' That number isn't a wild guess: it's the model's own learned estimate, the output it was trained to produce. But what does 80 percent even mean for one person, who either has pneumonia or doesn't? You can't judge it on a single patient. Think of a weather forecaster: if they say '80 percent chance of rain' on 100 days, it should actually rain on about 80 of them. Same here — we gather every patient the model called '80 percent' and check that roughly 80 percent really had the condition. That's calibration. ECE ('Expected Calibration Error') rolls this up into one number: it sorts patients into groups by the confidence they were given, measures how far each group's promised percentage was from what actually happened, and averages those gaps across the groups. Smaller is better; near zero means honest. And where did the model's sense of '80 percent' come from? We split our past patients into two piles: a study pile it learns from, and a hidden pile it never sees during learning. We grade only on the hidden pile — a real pop quiz — so we're measuring whether it truly learned the patterns, not whether it just memorized cases it had already seen. This all matters because the doctor will lean on that confidence number to decide how seriously to take a suggestion.
Why all three together?
Each report card catches something the others miss. Recall asks 'does the shortlist work the way a doctor actually uses it?' AUPRC asks 'are we good at the rare, important stuff, not just the easy cases?' Calibration asks 'can the doctor trust the confidence numbers?' A model could ace one and still be dangerous — great recall but wildly overconfident numbers, say. Since this tool supports real clinical decisions and isn't a fun demo, we need all three to look good at once.
Think of it like…
Grading the model is like grading a brilliant, super-fast new intern with three report cards instead of one: did the real diagnoses make it onto their shortlist, do they catch the rare dangers and not just the obvious cases, and — like a trustworthy weather forecaster whose "80% chance of rain" days actually rain about 80% of the time — is their confidence honest?

Step 1 of 6

The Big Picture

The moment a patient is admitted, M-02 — a computer model that learned from huge numbers of past patients — turns everything already known about them into one honest number: their chance of not surviving this hospital stay.

Picture a busy hospital on a Friday night. Over a short stretch, twenty patients are admitted — formally taken in to stay, not just passing through the waiting room — and there are only a handful of doctors to watch over all of them. Nobody can check on everyone at once, so someone has to decide who to look at first. That's the exact moment M-02 is built for.

So what is M-02? It's a computer program — an AI model that learned by studying the records of a huge number of past patients whose outcomes are already known. The instant a new patient is admitted, it looks at what's on file about them right then: their age; how they arrived (by ambulance, which often signals someone sicker, versus walking in on their own); their vital signs (basic body measurements like blood pressure and heart rate); illnesses recorded during their past hospital stays; and early lab results (tests run on samples like blood in a laboratory). Vitals and labs are both just measurements taken right around the time of arrival, before any treatment has started. From all of that, M-02 answers one specific question: "What's the chance this person dies during this hospital stay — this single visit, from being admitted to leaving?"

Notice it gives a chance, not a verdict. It won't say "this patient will die" or "this one is fine." It hands back a number — say 14% — and sorts that number into a simple color-coded bucket, so a busy doctor can glance at it and instantly know who to rush to first.

It answers one narrow question
Not "what's wrong with this patient?" but simply "how likely is it that they don't survive this stay?" — using only what's known the moment they're admitted (the "front door" of the hospital stay), before any treatment has started.
How it actually comes up with the number
M-02 isn't guessing, and it isn't following a doctor's rulebook. Beforehand it studied the records of a huge number of past patients — their age, vitals, history, and labs, plus whether they survived — and learned which combinations tended to end badly. For a new patient, it matches their pattern against everything it learned and reports how often past patients who looked like this one didn't survive. That's where the percentage comes from.
The answer is a probability, not a prophecy
A probability is just a chance out of 100. A 14% risk means "out of 100 patients who look like this one, about 14 don't survive the stay." It's the odds for people like them, never a guarantee about this one person.
The number becomes a traffic light
That raw percentage is sorted into four easy buckets — Low, Medium, High, Critical. A doctor doesn't have to interpret a decimal; they just see the bucket and know how urgent it is.
Really, it's a tireless triage assistant
Triage means sorting patients by how urgently they need care. Without it, people tend to get seen in the order they arrived — but the first to arrive isn't always the sickest. M-02 flags the highest-risk patients so they jump the queue, like a triage nurse who never gets tired.
The rare-death problem makes it tricky
Dying in the hospital is thankfully rare — only about 6 out of every 100 admissions. That's actually a hard setup: a lazy tool that just guessed "everyone survives" would be right about 94 times out of 100 while being completely useless. So M-02 is deliberately tuned to pay extra attention to those rare bad cases — during its training, missing a death is treated as far more costly than a false alarm — instead of chasing a high overall score.
Think of it like…
Think of a weather forecast. A forecast never promises "it will rain today" — it says "there's a 30% chance of rain." M-02 works exactly the same way. It doesn't declare who lives or dies; it gives a chance-of-rain for each patient, so people can carry an umbrella (call a senior doctor) when the odds look bad.

Step 2 of 6

What Goes In, What Comes Out

M-02 is a computer model that reads a quick snapshot of a patient at the door and turns it into one honest number: their chance of dying during this hospital stay.

Picture a nurse at the front desk, scribbling a quick snapshot of a patient the moment they walk in. That snapshot is exactly what M-02 reads. (M-02 is just the project's nickname for the computer model — no hidden meaning, like a car's model number.) It doesn't wait for a full workup or for the stay to unfold. It works with what's known right at the door.

Out the other end comes a single number: this patient's estimated chance of dying during this hospital stay — that is, before they're discharged. One clean percentage, like "14%."

So that's the whole trade. A quick snapshot goes in. One honest number comes out. Let's walk through both sides.

The inputs: a quick intake snapshot
M-02 uses six everyday things, all noted at admission. The patient's age. How they arrived — rushed in through the emergency room versus a planned, scheduled visit (emergency arrivals tend to be sicker). Recent vital signs, which are quick body readings like blood pressure and BMI (a simple weight-to-height ratio). Health problems recorded during past hospital visits. Any early lab results, if any exist yet. And one overall sickness score — which is the Charlson score I'll explain in a moment. Nothing exotic. It's the same stuff a nurse jots on an intake form.
The Charlson score: a credit score for your health
That last input — the one overall sickness score — is called the Charlson score, and it deserves its own line. Think of it like a credit score, but for long-term health. It rolls up a person's chronic conditions — ongoing illnesses that don't simply go away, like diabetes or heart disease — counting how many they have and how serious each one is, into a single trusted number. It saves the model from re-learning something medicine figured out long ago.
Only what's known at the door — no peeking ahead
Every input is frozen at the moment of admission. To be precise about the trickiest one: the vital signs and labs come from readings taken as the patient arrives or from just before — the cutoff line is drawn exactly at admission, and nothing measured after it is allowed in. Why so strict? Because the whole point is to help right now, at the door. If the tool secretly used a test result from three days into the stay, it would look brilliant in testing but be useless in real life — because at the moment you actually need the guess, none of that future information exists yet. So it works from a closed book.
How can a usually-blank input still count?
Here's a surprise: about 88% of lab results are simply missing when someone is admitted. The model doesn't fake a number to fill the gap, and it doesn't throw the patient out either. Instead, 'missing' becomes its own official answer — a separate category the model was trained to recognize, sitting right alongside 'high' or 'low.' Because it learned from huge numbers of past patients, it already saw how people with that lab left blank tended to fare, and it leans on that. A blank can even be a clue: a missing lab might mean 'just arrived, tests not started yet' or 'wasn't urgent enough to order,' and both hint at risk. The same goes for the other inputs — a patient who's never been hospitalized before simply has an empty history, and that emptiness ('none on record') is itself treated as meaningful, not as a mistake.
The output: one honest percentage
Out comes a single number between 0 and 100% — say, 14%. And it means what it sounds like: of past patients who looked like this one, roughly 14 in 100 died during the stay. This isn't a hunch that someone dressed up with a number. It's built by counting what actually happened to real past patients. And it's checked: the makers took thousands of past cases, gathered everyone the tool had rated '14%,' and confirmed that about 14 of every 100 of them really did die. Wherever the tool's percentages drifted from what actually happened, they tuned it until the two lined up. That tuning is exactly why a '14%' can be trusted to mean roughly 14%.
From number to action: the traffic-light buckets
A busy nurse shouldn't have to do math mid-shift, so that percentage drops into one of four bands: Low, Medium, High, or Critical (see the table). Each band triggers a different response. Low means the normal queue. Medium means standard care but with closer eyes — staff recheck the patient's vitals more often and re-run the tool as new labs arrive. High means a senior doctor is asked to review the patient soon, ahead of the usual line. Critical means a senior doctor is pulled in immediately, dropping other non-urgent tasks. And a fair question: what about a patient who doesn't resemble anyone in the past data? The tool still gives a number, but it's most reliable for common cases and shakier for unusual ones — which is exactly why it only flags patients for a human to judge, and never makes the call itself.
Think of it like…
It's like the weather app on your phone. It looks at today's conditions — temperature, clouds, wind — and says "14% chance of rain." M-02 does the same with a patient's condition at the door: it reads the snapshot and says "14% chance of dying during this stay." And just like a real forecast, it can only use what it can see right now. It's never allowed to peek at how tomorrow actually turns out.

Step 3 of 6

Why We Chose This Model

We picked a model built for messy, blank-filled tables and rare outcomes, because that is exactly what a patient's data looks like the moment they walk in.

The moment a patient is admitted, one of the system's models — its internal name is M-02, and its one job is estimating in-hospital mortality — hands the care team a single number: the chance this person could die during this hospital stay. (It works alongside sister models that flag other risks, like the odds of needing intensive care.) Think of that number like a weather forecast. It is not a promise of rain, just "14% chance." It helps the team decide who to rush to first, even when intake is crowded and busy.

So the real question in this section is not whether a computer can make that guess. It is which tool should make it. And honestly, the data decided for us.

Our data has two stubborn quirks — plus one hard constraint that shadows both: the patient has only just arrived, so there is barely any history to lean on yet. Once you see all three, the right choice (and why the obvious alternatives fall short) falls right into place.

Quirk one: dying is rare
Only about 5 or 6 of every 100 admitted patients die during the stay — 5.6%, to be exact — so about 94 of every 100 survive. That makes this an "imbalanced" problem, which just means one answer (survives) shows up far more often than the other (dies). Here is the trap: a lazy model can be "right" about 94% of the time simply by blindly guessing "everyone survives," while completely failing at the one job we care about — spotting the few who will not. So we need a tool we can force to take the rare cases seriously.
Quirk two: most of the boxes are blank
When someone is admitted, the model would like to read a whole panel of early lab results — blood counts, kidney numbers, and so on. But across all of those lab values, about 87.6% come up blank at the moment of admission — only about 1 in 8 admissions arrives with any early labs already back. Picture a health checklist where most boxes are empty on most patients. You cannot simply pencil in "normal" for the blanks. Most blanks really are boring — the result just is not back yet — but a blank is never safe to read as "healthy," because whether a test got ordered and resulted before admission is tied to how the patient showed up, not to random chance. (Sometimes, for instance, a test is missing because the team was worried enough to start treating before waiting on a routine result.) So we need a tool that can treat "this is blank" as its own kind of information and let the historical data decide whether it matters — rather than one that guesses "normal" and throws that signal away.
Our pick: a relay of tiny yes/no trees
We chose gradient-boosted decision trees. (The exact version carries a clunky name, HistGradientBoosting — I will unpack the "Hist" in a second.) Start with the pieces. A decision tree is just a chain of yes/no questions: "Age over 70? Blood pressure low?" One tree alone is a weak guesser. "Boosting" stacks hundreds of them into a relay — and that stacking happens during training, a word worth pinning down: training is the one-time learning phase where the model studies a huge pile of past admissions whose outcomes we already know (who lived, who died) and adjusts itself to match. In that phase, the first tree makes a rough guess, the next studies exactly where the first went wrong and drills only those misses, and the next cleans up whatever is still off. The "gradient" part is simply the math for which way, and how hard, to nudge: each new tree is handed not just which cases are wrong but the direction and size of every miss — like reading the slope of a hill to find the fastest way down — and steps the running total that way. No human writes the questions; the computer auto-tries thousands and keeps whichever ones best separate survivors from non-survivors. At the end, each tree drops a small nudge into a pot, you add them all up, and one fixed S-shaped curve — called the logistic function — bends that total (which could land at any number at all) onto a clean 0-to-100% scale. And the "Hist"? It means the tool first sorts each measurement into a handful of buckets (a histogram) instead of weighing every exact value — a shortcut that lets it train fast on an ordinary CPU, which is all the hardware we have.
It treats a blank as a clue — and nobody taught it that
Here is the neat part. "Is this lab result blank?" is simply one more of the thousands of yes/no questions the computer tries during training, no different from "age over 70?" If blank boxes line up with more deaths in the historical records, the model keeps that question as a useful rule. "Learning" here does not mean the computer understands medicine. It is more like a detective working through case file after case file — in our case, all 546,028 past hospital admissions where we already know who survived — and noticing "when this box is blank, the patient turned out to have died more often." A pattern kept only because it kept matching real outcomes we already knew.
Two extra nudges to match our own patients
On top of the trees, we do two small things. First, "sample weighting": during training we hand the rare death cases extra weight, so the model cannot dismiss them as noise — and "noise" here means random, one-off variation that carries no real repeatable pattern. The weighting forces the model to treat those few deaths as signal worth learning, not flukes to smooth over. Second, we feed it the Charlson Comorbidity Index. "Comorbidity" means the other serious illnesses a patient already carries alongside whatever brought them in — diabetes, heart failure, kidney disease, cancer. The Charlson index is a decades-old, widely trusted scoring system that assigns points to each such condition and adds them into a single number that has predicted mortality well for about 40 years. Handing that to the model is like giving a new hire a cheat-sheet a veteran already wrote, on top of everything the model figures out on its own.
The honesty check that makes it safe to act on
The final step is "isotonic calibration." The idea is simple: if the model says 14%, do the patients it labels 14% really die about 14% of the time? If not, we gently bend the numbers until they do. "Isotonic" just means "only ever goes one way" — the adjustment can slide a score up or down, but it must never flip the ranking, so a patient the model ranked riskier stays ranked riskier. This matters enormously, because the numbers trigger real actions. In this system, crossing about 5% flags a patient to watch more closely; about 10% escalates them immediately, jumping the queue so a clinician is pulled in; about 25% marks them high/critical, top of the pile. A dishonest number would pull clinicians toward patients who are actually fine, and away from the ones in real danger. And yes — because the low 5% "watch" bar sits right around the 5.6% average risk, it fires fairly often. That is fine: "watch" is cheap. The expensive action, actually pulling a clinician off other work, is gated behind the rarer 10% and 25% bars.
Why not something simpler?
Every obvious alternative trips over one of our two quirks — or over that hard constraint, the just-arrived patient with almost no history yet. The table below lays them side by side. The short version: the simpler tools are easier to read or already trusted, but each one is either less accurate than it could be, or needs data we simply do not have at the front door.
Think of it like…
Picture a study group cramming for one brutal exam. The first tutor takes a swing and gets some questions right and some wrong. Instead of starting over, the second tutor is handed only the questions the first one blew, and drills exactly those. The third cleans up whatever is still wrong. Stack a few hundred tutors like this, each one obsessed with the leftover mistakes, and the group nails a test no single tutor could pass alone. That is "boosting," and it is precisely how our model is built — except a computer plays every tutor, automatically, in seconds.

Step 4 of 6

What Else Could We Use

We had other options, but each was either too simple, too generic, or needed data we don't have yet — so we picked the model that handles missing labs and gives numbers doctors can trust.

Quick reminder: M-02 is our shorthand for "Model 02," the tool that predicts a patient's risk of dying during this hospital stay. It looks at them the moment they arrive, gives one number — say, a 14% chance — then drops them into a bucket (Low, Medium, High, Critical) so the sickest people get a clinician's eyes first. To do that, we chose a method called HistGradientBoosting (an odd name we'll unpack near the end).

But that wasn't the only tool we could have reached for. Three other approaches were on the table, and each is worth knowing — because seeing why they lost out is the clearest way to understand why our pick won.

The short version: the simpler tools were easier to explain but less accurate and clumsier with missing data, and the fanciest AI needed a kind of data we just don't have the instant someone walks in. Here's each one, in plain language.

Option 1: The add-up-the-points checklist
The first option, called logistic regression, works like a risk checklist. Over 65? Add 2 points. Diabetes? Add 1. Low blood pressure? Add 3. Total the points, turn it into a percentage. Its charm is that you can read it — a doctor sees exactly why a patient got flagged. But it's less accurate. It scored about 82-84 on a 0-to-100 measure called AUROC. Careful, though: AUROC is NOT 'percent of patients it got right.' It's a ranking score — line up one patient who died and one who lived, and it's the chance the model gives the sicker one the higher risk number. 50 means pure coin-flip and 100 means flawless, so in real medicine the useful range is squeezed into roughly the 70s-to-90s — which is why beating 82-84 by even 2 or 3 points, as our pick reliably does, is a genuine gap, not rounding noise. Its other weakness shows up with missing data. About 88 of every 100 patients arrive with at least one lab not yet drawn, and a checklist can't just leave a blank — it substitutes a stand-in number, usually the average. Say a patient's potassium (a salt in the blood that, at the wrong level, can disturb the heartbeat) was never measured. You plug in the 'average,' which looks perfectly normal — but this particular patient was actually in danger there. The model now treats them as fine on that item, and the error slips in silently.
Option 2: The trusted paper scorecards
Next were the bedside scores clinicians already use — APACHE II (Acute Physiology And Chronic Health Evaluation, version 2), NEWS2 (National Early Warning Score, version 2), and SOFA (Sequential Organ Failure Assessment). These are hand-built point systems: read the patient's vital signs (heart rate, breathing rate, temperature, blood pressure, oxygen level, alertness) plus a few blood-test values off a printed chart, add up the points, done — no computer needed. They're trusted and fast. But they're one-size-fits-all rules, and some are genuinely old: APACHE II dates to 1985 and SOFA to the mid-1990s. None were tuned to our own records — and to be clear, 'our patients' doesn't mean one specific building. It means the large historical database of past, de-identified hospital admissions this project learns from. It's like grabbing a generic shoe-size chart instead of measuring your actual foot: fine in a pinch, but a custom fit does better. And a paper score never gets sharper as more records pile up. Ours does.
Option 3: The fancy AI (deep learning)
Then there's deep learning — the powerful 'transformer' models behind ChatGPT-style systems. ('Transformer' is just the name of the design; its trick is reading a whole sequence at once and weighing how each item relates to all the others — which event gives meaning to the rest.) They shine on long, ordered sequences. For a patient, that 'sequence' would be their medical timeline: visit after visit, diagnosis after diagnosis, drug after drug, stretching back over months or years. The catch: M-02 has to decide the instant a patient walks in, from a single snapshot — age, vital signs, past diagnoses, maybe one early lab. There's no long timeline to read yet. Bringing a novel-reader to a job that only hands you a business card is overkill, so we set it aside for now. It may well fit other parts of this project that DO read a patient's full history over time — for instance, the piece that walks through every one of a patient's prior admissions in order — but not this arrival-moment predictor.
Meet the winner: what the name actually means
Our pick's full name is HistGradientBoosting, and it's less scary once you split it. 'Boosting' means it builds many small yes/no flowcharts one after another, and each new one is trained to fix the leftover mistakes of all the ones before it. 'Gradient' is just the math trick for measuring which direction those mistakes point, so the next flowchart knows which way to nudge. 'Hist' (short for histogram) is a speed trick: instead of testing every possible cutoff for, say, age, it sorts the values into a few bins (0-10, 10-20, and so on) and only checks those — far fewer options, much faster. Here's the boosting idea in miniature. Suppose the running guess for everyone starts at 10%. For a patient who actually died, 10% was too low, so that patient leaves behind an 'error' the next flowchart is told to shrink — it learns a rule that pushes similar patients' numbers up. Do this hundreds of times and the running total creeps toward the truth. And when a flowchart chooses WHERE to split — 'age over 70?' — 'better' has a concrete meaning: after the split, is each branch more lopsided toward one outcome? If the 'over 70' branch ends up mostly patients who died and the other branch mostly survivors, that split earned its place; a split that leaves both branches a 50/50 mix is useless and gets thrown away.
Superpower 1: it shrugs off blanks
That flowchart design hands us the first big advantage: missing labs don't trip it up, and it never fakes a value. At a fork like 'is potassium above 4.0?' — potassium being that blood salt from earlier, measured in millimoles per litre, where a normal level sits around 3.5-5.0, so 4.0 is a middle-of-the-road cutoff — the model also learns a third path for 'this test was never done.' How does it decide where the not-tested patients go? It literally tries sending them down each branch on past data and keeps whichever choice better separates who died from who lived (that same lopsidedness test from a moment ago). So a blank stops being a hole to paper over and becomes its own answer. And that's often genuinely informative: 'nobody ordered this test' can quietly signal the patient didn't look sick enough to warrant it yet.
Superpower 2: honest percentages — and why they decide everything
The second advantage is calibration — making sure '14%' really means about 14-in-100 in real life, not just 'riskier than the person next to them.' You test it exactly like grading a weather forecaster: gather every patient the model called 'about 14%,' then count how many actually died. Close to 14 in 100? Honest. Only 3 in 100? It's crying wolf. A scary 30 in 100? It's dangerously underplaying risk. If the model comes out miscalibrated, you don't retrain it from scratch — you add a correction step. On a batch of patients it never trained on, you compare what it predicted to what really happened and build a little translation table ('whenever it says 30%, reality is closer to 20% — so shrink it'), then apply that table to every future score. Why sweat this? Because the raw percentage is what sorts patients into the four action buckets, using cutoffs the team sets by hand: below 5% is Low, 5-10% is Medium, 10-25% is High, above 25% is Critical. Those thresholds are a human policy choice — set to catch the genuinely sick without drowning staff in false alarms — not something the model invents. And a bucket isn't a diagnosis or an automatic order; it's a flag that moves a High or Critical patient up a clinician's queue so a human looks sooner. A number off by 10 points could bump someone into the wrong bucket — which is exactly why the honesty matters.
Think of it like…
Picking a model is like picking a vehicle for a specific road. A bicycle is simple and you can see exactly how every part works, but it's slow. A borrowed generic sedan is reliable, but it was never tuned for your particular route. A private jet is astonishingly powerful, yet useless on a short, bumpy road with no runway. We needed something built for the actual terrain — a rugged truck that keeps rolling even when the road is full of potholes (the missing lab values) — so that's what we chose.

Step 5 of 6

How We Know It Works

We grade M-02 on three separate report cards, because being great at one of them doesn't make it safe on the others.

Meet M-02. The name is just our in-house label — "M" for Model, "02" because it's one of several versions we've built and compared. Plainly put, it's a computer program that studied huge numbers of past hospital records — learning which mixes of age, vital signs, past illnesses, and early lab results tended to end with a patient dying during their stay — and now uses those learned patterns to score each new patient. Think of it as a triage nurse who never sleeps: the second someone is admitted, it reads their age, vitals, history, and early labs, then produces one number — say, a 14% chance of dying during this stay. ("Risk number," "percentage," "chance," and "probability" all mean that same single output here.) That number then drops into a color bucket — Low, Medium, High, or Critical — that helps decide who gets seen first.

But a number is easy to produce and hard to trust. "Just trust me" doesn't cut it when a life is on the line. So instead of taking the model's word for it, we grade it on three separate report cards — each one answering a different worry about whether it's actually any good.

Here are all three, in plain English, and why we need every one of them.

AUROC — can it sort the sick from the healthy?
Line up every patient from healthiest to sickest by the model's risk number. AUROC (say it "A-U-R-O-C") plays a simple game: pick one patient who actually died and one who actually survived — did the model give the one who died the higher risk? Now, the "how often" part: we don't do this just once. We run every possible pairing — each patient who died matched against each patient who survived — and AUROC is simply the fraction of those match-ups the model gets the right way round. A score of 0.50 means it's no better than a coin flip (useless); 1.00 is perfect. We aim for above 0.85 — that's roughly where the ranking gets sharp enough to actually trust for triage, and it's in line with what strong published hospital-mortality models reach, so we're holding ourselves to a real bar, not a flattering one. The name spells out "Area Under the ROC Curve," and yes, there's a real curve behind it. Picture sliding the alarm threshold from strict to lenient; at each setting you plot two things — the share of real deaths you correctly flag, against the share of survivors you falsely flag — and connect the dots into a curve. The area beneath that curve (a number from 0 to 1) turns out to equal exactly the pairwise odds we just described. Same thing, two ways of seeing it. Why care about ranking at all? Because for triage, the ORDER is what matters most. Even if the exact percentage is a touch off, as long as the sickest people land at the front of the line, doctors see the right patients first.
Calibration (ECE) — does "14%" really mean 14%?
AUROC only checks the order, not whether the number itself is honest. Calibration checks the honesty. Think of a weather forecaster: if she says "70% chance of rain" on ten different days, it should actually rain on about seven of them. Same test here — out of everyone the model tagged "around 14% risk," did about 14% really die? ECE stands for Expected Calibration Error. It's just the technical name for the average size of the gap between the promised percentage and what actually happened; smaller is better, and 0 would mean the model's numbers are perfectly honest. This one is non-negotiable, because M-02 uses hard cut-offs to auto-decide who gets rushed to a doctor: under 5% is Low, 5–10% Medium, 10–25% High, over 25% Critical. "Hard" just means the line is absolute — 4.9% lands in Low and 5.1% in Medium, with no gray zone or human judgment softening it in between; crossing the line flips the label, full stop. (Three dividing lines — 5%, 10%, 25% — always carve the range into four buckets, the way three cuts across a rope leave you four pieces; Critical is simply everything past the last cut.) If "14%" secretly meant "30%," patients would be dangerously under-escalated — parked in a calmer bucket than their real risk deserves.
But how do you group patients who are all "around the same 14%"?
Fair question — almost nobody scores exactly 14.000%. So we don't group by an exact match; we group by neighborhood. It's like sorting laundry: socks don't have to be the identical shade of gray to go in the gray pile, just close enough. Here's how the piles are actually drawn: we chop the whole 0–100% range into equal-width bands, decided in advance — a common choice is ten bands of 10 points each (0–10%, 10–20%, …), or twenty bands of 5 points. The width is fixed and announced up front, so it's not a matter of taste: anyone using the same band scheme sorts the patients into the same piles and gets the same answer. (The number of bands is a knob we can turn, but we state which setting we used, so nobody can quietly cherry-pick a flattering one.) Because we run this check on PAST patients whose outcome we already know, we can peek inside each pile and count: of the 400 patients in the "around 14%" band, did about 56 (14%) really die? If yes, that pile is honest. If 120 died, it's lying. ECE then combines those gaps across all the piles — but not with a plain average: each pile counts in proportion to how many patients it holds, so a crowded pile of 5,000 moves the score far more than a sparse pile of 5. That way the grade reflects where most patients actually land.
AUPRC — does it catch the rare deaths without crying wolf?
Here's the tricky part: dying in the hospital is rare — in the records M-02 learned from, only about 5.6 of every 100 admissions ended in death. (That's a fact about this particular dataset, not a universal law of hospitals — other hospitals and other years can run higher or lower.) That imbalance — far more survivors than deaths — sets a trap. A lazy model could look "94% accurate" just by always shouting "everyone survives," like a smoke detector that never goes off in a house that never catches fire: it scores perfectly precisely because it does nothing. Useless. AUPRC sidesteps that trap by asking two honest questions at once — and each has its own name: - When the model sounds the alarm, is it usually right (few false alarms)? That's called precision. - Of all the real deaths, how many did it actually catch (few misses)? That's called recall (also known as sensitivity). These are two separate ideas worth remembering by name, because a model can be strong on one and weak on the other. The name means "Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve" — same trick as AUROC. Slide the alarm threshold from strict to lenient, plot precision against recall at each setting, connect the dots, and measure the area beneath (again a number from 0 to 1). A high area means precision AND recall stay up together: the model catches the rare deaths without flooding doctors with false alarms — the exact failure we fear most in triage.
Why all three, together — and why a great AUROC isn't enough on its own
No single number tells the whole story, and a model can ace one card while flunking another. Here's the concrete reason a great AUROC doesn't guarantee honest percentages: ranking only cares about order. Suppose three patients' true risks are 7%, 14%, and 21%, but the model reports 14%, 28%, and 42% — every number doubled. The ORDER is still perfect (AUROC is thrilled), yet every percentage is a lie (calibration is terrible). Because you can stretch or shrink all the numbers by the same factor without changing their order, a model can sort patients flawlessly while its percentages run wildly high or low — and those percentages are exactly what the 5% / 10% / 25% cut-offs act on. That's why order-checking alone isn't safe. Using all three cards together is like checking a bathroom scale is both precise (same reading every time) AND accurate (matches your true weight). One without the other isn't good enough when the stakes are a patient's life.
Two things we baked into M-02 so it can pass all three
Everything above is how we GRADE M-02 after the fact. Two choices we made while BUILDING it help it earn those grades — and they're easy to confuse with the tests, so here's the difference plainly. Weighting (for the rare-deaths problem). While the model is learning, we tell it that missing a real death hurts far more than raising a false alarm — in effect, each rare death "counts" like many patients instead of one. This stops it from taking the lazy "everyone survives" shortcut just because deaths are so outnumbered. Weighting is a training instruction, not a grade: it shapes what the model tries to get right, whereas AUPRC just measures how well it ended up doing. A calibration step (not the same as the calibration test). ECE is the grade for honesty; the calibration step is the fix that earns the grade. After the model produces its raw scores, we pass them through a small adjustment (fitted on past data) that gently nudges the numbers so "around 14%" truly lands near a 14% real-world death rate. So: the step adjusts the percentages, and ECE then checks whether that adjustment actually worked.
Think of it like…
Judging M-02 is like judging a weather forecaster. A good one does three things: she ranks rainy days above dry ones (that's AUROC), she keeps her percentages honest — a "70% chance" should mean rain about 7 days out of 10 (that's calibration, graded by ECE), and she still nails the rare, dangerous storms instead of missing them or crying wolf (that's AUPRC). Miss any one of the three, and you shouldn't trust the forecast — or the model.

The Big Picture

M-03 (the hospital's ICU-risk tool) looks at a patient the moment they arrive and estimates how likely they are to need intensive care, so staff can get ready early instead of scrambling later.

Picture a hospital's front desk on a busy night. Fifty patients arrive — some on their own feet, some by ambulance. Most just need a normal bed and normal checks. But a few are quietly heading for trouble: they look okay right now, yet at some point during their stay they'll need the most intensive care the hospital has — the ICU (intensive care unit, where one nurse watches just one or two patients constantly, instead of one nurse spread across ten). For some, that turn comes within hours; for others, a day or two into the stay.

Here's the catch. Today, hospitals usually find out someone needs the ICU only after they've already gotten sicker. A doctor examines them, orders more tests, and only then asks for an ICU bed. That can take hours. And ICU beds are scarce — each one has to be prepped with staff and equipment — so every hour of delay hurts both the patient and the planning.

M-03's whole job is to make that call right at the front door. ("M-03" is simply this project's internal name for the model — it's the third in a numbered lineup of tools, so the "03" is just a label, not a score or a version number you need to decode.) It gives a chance, not a yes-or-no
M-03 never says "ICU: yes" or "ICU: no." It gives a percentage, like a weather forecast saying "70% chance of rain" — here it's "this patient has an X% chance of needing ICU-level care during this stay." For context, only about 13 out of every 100 people admitted actually end up in the ICU, so the tool is hunting for that small group early, before they visibly stand out.
The percentage becomes a simple traffic light
A raw number isn't much use to a busy bed team — the staff (typically a charge nurse plus bed managers) whose job is deciding which patient goes in which bed. So M-03 turns the number into a green / yellow / red signal. Green: a regular ward bed is fine. Yellow: put them in a step-down unit — one notch below the ICU, where nurses re-take vital signs and look in on the patient far more often and they stay on continuous monitors, but without the full one-nurse-per-patient intensity of the ICU. Red: request an ICU bed now, before the doctor has even finished confirming it. The 15% and 40% cutoffs aren't magic numbers — they're a judgment call about how cautious the hospital wants to be. Notice that 15% sits just above the 13% average, so "green" really means "no riskier than a typical arriving patient," while "red" at 40% flags someone whose risk is roughly triple the average.
It only uses what's known at the front door
M-03 looks at just the information available on arrival: age and sex; how the patient got there (on foot, by ambulance, or transferred from another facility — including people who arrive unconscious and can't answer questions, in which case it works from whatever the paramedics recorded and simply gives a less confident estimate); vital signs (the quick bedside measurements — heart rate, blood pressure, temperature); basic body measurements like height and weight; conditions recorded during the patient's earlier admissions to this hospital system (from stays that ended before this one — not routine outpatient checkups); and the first lab results (values from blood and other samples sent to the lab). It deliberately ignores anything that happens after admission — and that's the whole point, not a limitation. At the real front door, that later information simply doesn't exist yet, so the tool has to prove it can predict without it. Letting it peek at later events while being built would make it look brilliant in testing but useless in practice — like a student who "aces" a practice test only because the answers were printed on the page, then fails the real exam where the answers are hidden. One more thing: this is a snapshot taken at arrival — the first read. If important new information comes in later, a fresh estimate can be run, but M-03's core job is that front-door number.
It's a careful guess — and it can be wrong
Because M-03 is estimating, it will sometimes miss. Two kinds of mistakes matter. A false alarm (red, but the patient turns out fine) can tie up a scarce ICU bed. A miss (green, but the patient crashes anyway) is the more dangerous one. That's why the percentage is "calibrated" — deliberately tuned so that a 40% score really does mean about 40 out of 100 such patients need the ICU, not 30 or 50. And red is a heads-up that lets the team get ready, not an order that overrides the doctor: a human still makes the final call.
Why it's worth building
Two very concrete payoffs. Speed for the patient: someone quietly heading for a crisis gets flagged and moved toward the right care hours earlier. Planning for the hospital: instead of hunting for an ICU bed at the last second, the bed team gets an early heads-up and can line up staff, equipment, and space in advance.

What Goes In, What Comes Out

M-03 is a small computer model that takes only what we know the moment a patient arrives and turns it into one number: the chance they'll need intensive care.

Picture a patient walking through the hospital doors. M-03 is a small computer program — it learned its patterns by studying thousands of past patients — and the instant a new patient arrives, it looks at everything we know about them and hands back a single number: the chance they'll need the ICU (Intensive Care Unit, the ward for the sickest patients, with one-on-one nursing, breathing machines, and around-the-clock monitoring).

How does it land on that number? It doesn't diagnose anyone. It compares this patient to all the past patients who arrived looking similar and asks a simple question — what fraction of them ended up in the ICU? That fraction is the number it reports.

The catch is what counts as "the moment they arrive." M-03 runs right at the front door: after the nurse's quick triage checks, but before the doctor has examined the patient and figured out what's wrong. What goes in — only front-door facts, and why each one matters

Past medical history — diseases diagnosed on earlier visits, like knowing a car's repair record before it pulls into the shop — because a patient with a weak heart or lungs is likelier to crash. And early labs: the fast blood tests, things like blood counts and kidney readings, that come back within about an hour. That's the whole menu — and a slower lab isn't banned for being slow, it simply hasn't come back yet at the moment M-03 has to decide.
What comes out — a probability, not a yes or no
The output is one number between 0 and 1, called a probability. Think of it like a weather forecast: '70% chance of rain' means that in situations like this one, it rains about 70 times out of 100. Here it reads '70% chance this patient will need the ICU.' It's a confidence level, not a promise.
What makes a 70% honest
Two things separate a trustworthy 70% from a worthless one. First, it has to be calibrated: when M-03 says 70%, roughly 70 of every 100 such patients really do end up in the ICU — the number matches what actually happens. Second, it has to be earned from front-door facts alone, with no peeking at what comes later. A model that secretly leaned on later information would look brilliant in testing and be useless at the real front door, where that future doesn't exist yet — like grading a weather forecaster after they've already looked out the window.
Why 'right on average' isn't good enough
In this hospital's own records — the past patients M-03 learned from — only about 13 of every 100 admitted patients actually end up in the ICU. So being 'roughly right on average' is easy and useless: a lazy system could guess 'no ICU' for everyone and still be right 87 times out of 100 (that's 100 minus 13). M-03's real job is to spot the right 13 early, before they've visibly gotten worse.

Why We Chose This Model

We chose a team of simple decision-trees that fix each other's mistakes, because it catches the risky combinations of factors a simpler formula would miss.

Imagine the hospital front desk. The second a patient is admitted, this tool whispers one number to the bed-management team: the chance this person will need intensive care (ICU) — the highest, most closely-watched level of care. If that number is high, the team can start lining up an ICU bed right now, instead of waiting hours for a doctor to make it official. About 13 of every 100 admissions actually end up needing the ICU, so we're hunting for a needle in a haystack.

Choosing the right method for that hunt is the whole game. This data has three awkward habits: the event is rare, the warning signs combine in sneaky ways, and a lot of information is simply missing at the door. Any method that trips over even one of those is out.

The winner was gradient-boosted decision trees — picture a relay team of simple flowchart-style doctors who correct each other — with three safety features bolted on. Here's why that beat both the simpler options and the fancier ones.

First, what the number is actually for
The tool hands the bed team a single probability, and that number quietly sorts patients. Under 15%, they head to a normal ward; between 15% and 40%, a step-down unit (a halfway level — watched more closely than a ward, less than full ICU) gets a heads-up; over 40%, the team starts requesting an ICU bed straight away. Those two cutoffs aren't the model's call — clinicians set them as hospital policy, trading scarce ICU beds against patient safety, and they can be moved without retraining anything. The low one sits at 15% on purpose, not by accident: since about 13 in 100 admissions genuinely need the ICU, anyone scoring even a little above that typical rate is worth a second look, while below it you're at or under the average and a routine ward is reasonable. Because the number physically moves beds around, it has to be trustworthy, not just roughly in the right ballpark.
The three things that make this hard
First, it's rare: a model that just says 'no ICU' for everyone is technically right 87% of the time but useless, like a smoke detector that never goes off. Second, the clues combine: older is mildly risky, a heart condition is mildly risky, but older AND a heart condition AND low blood pressure together is far riskier than adding them up would suggest. Third, lots of info is missing, especially for first-time patients with no chart on file. Whatever we pick has to survive all three at once.
Our pick: a relay of self-correcting trees
A decision tree is just a flowchart of yes/no questions ('blood pressure below 100? age over 65?'). But each path through the flowchart doesn't dead-end in a plain 'yes ICU / no ICU' — it ends in a little number, a nudge like +0.3 or -0.5, stored at the tip of that branch. Now the 'boosting' part: we chain hundreds of these trees together. The first tree makes a rough guess for everyone. We then measure, for each patient, how far that guess sits from what actually happened — that leftover gap between guess and reality is the error. The second tree isn't asked to predict ICU-or-not from scratch; it's trained to predict that leftover error, so it automatically zooms in on the patients the first tree botched. The third cleans up what's still off, and so on. ('Gradient' is simply the math word for that leftover gap — which direction each guess is off, and by how much; every new tree is aimed straight at it. So the full name decodes as: gradient = the error to chase, boosting = the relay of fixers.) To get one final answer, we start from a baseline and add up every tree's little nudge, then squeeze that running total through a simple conversion that turns any number into a value between 0% and 100% — and that's the percentage the bed team sees. Stacked up this way, the trees capture the sneaky combination risks a single rule can't, and they run on ordinary computers. (A close cousin called XGBoost — the exact same gradient-boosted-trees idea, just a faster, more heavily optimized engine for it — sits in our back pocket if we ever need more speed or power.)
Safety feature 1: paying attention to the rare cases (sample-weighting)
Left alone, the model would notice that guessing 'not ICU' is almost always safe and get lazy about the rare, dangerous cases — the exact ones we care about. Here's the concrete fix: during training we make each ICU case count for more, telling the model to treat one real ICU patient as if it were several, so a mistake on that patient costs it many times more points than a mistake on an ordinary ward patient. Since the whole model is built to lose as few points as possible, it can no longer afford to breeze past the rare cases. It's like an exam where the few hard questions are each worth ten marks — suddenly you can't ignore them. This is the direct fix for the needle-in-a-haystack problem.
Safety feature 2: honest percentages (calibration)
There are two very different questions here, easy to mix up. Ranking asks: did we put the sicker patients above the less-sick ones? Calibration asks: are the actual percentages honest? You can nail the order while the numbers are nonsense — three patients whose true risks are 5%, 20%, and 60% could be scored 1%, 2%, 3%: perfect order, wildly wrong numbers. Fine for a leaderboard, dangerous when the number moves a bed. Calibration fixes this: if the model says '30%,' then out of 100 such patients about 30 really should need the ICU. Tree models rank well by default, but their raw scores aren't honest percentages yet. So here's what 'stretching' them actually means: after training, we take a fresh batch of patients the model has never seen, line up its raw scores against how many of those patients truly went to the ICU, and fit a simple correction curve — a bendy line that says, for example, 'whenever this model outputs 0.30, history shows the real rate was 28%, so report 28%.' Applying that curve to every future score is the stretch: it bends the raw numbers up or down until they match reality, without ever disturbing their order.
Safety feature 3: a common-sense leash (monotonic constraints)
'Monotonic' just means 'moves in one direction only — never doubles back.' A monotonic constraint is a built-in sanity rule of exactly that shape: we force the model to agree that a few inputs can only ever push the estimated risk up as they increase, never down. We apply it to three: age; the number of past hospital stays; and a comorbidity index — a single number (the well-known Charlson score) that rolls up how many serious long-term conditions a patient already carries into one 'overall pre-existing-sickness' figure. Locking these to one direction stops the model from ever learning something backwards, like 'being older somehow lowered the risk.' Even if that glitch showed up only rarely, a doctor who spotted it would stop trusting the whole tool — so we forbid it up front.
What '42%' really means for one person
Here's a subtle truth: you can never check whether a single person's '42%' was right. They either needed the ICU or they didn't, and you can't rerun their stay 100 times. The 42% isn't really about that one patient — it describes everyone who looks like them on the clues we can see: 'out of a large group arriving like this, about 42 in 100 end up needing the ICU.' We verify it after the fact by gathering every patient scored around 40–45% and checking what fraction actually went to the ICU, the same way you'd grade a weather forecaster over a whole year rather than on one day.
What the model does when info is missing
It does NOT quietly plug in an average or a zero. That matters because the simpler methods usually can't tolerate a blank at all — you must fill the hole before they'll even run, typically with the column's average or a plain 0, which invents a reading the patient never had and quietly feeds the model a fiction. The trees skip that trick entirely. As each tree is built, at a question like 'is blood pressure below 100?' the algorithm mechanically tries it both ways: it sends every patient whose blood pressure is missing down the 'yes' branch and measures how cleanly ICU and non-ICU patients end up separated, then sends them all down the 'no' branch and measures again. Whichever direction sorts the real training patients more cleanly becomes a stored rule: 'if this value is missing, send the patient this way.' There's no little internal conversation — it's just try-both-splits-and-keep-the-better-one, counted on real data. The catch: a total stranger with almost no history trips nearly every 'if-missing' rule at once, so the model falls back on the few real clues it has (age, the first vitals) and drifts toward the average patient. That's exactly why it's less sharp for first-timers.
How we check it's actually good
Three separate ways. First, ranking quality, measured by AUROC — roughly, how often it correctly orders a riskier patient above a less-risky one. That score runs from 0.5, which is pure coin-flip guessing, up to 1.0, which is flawless; so our target of above 0.80 sits well clear of the useless 0.5 floor, and a gap like 0.80 versus 0.75 is a real, meaningful difference, not a rounding wobble. Second, calibration, because these percentages route real beds. Third, and reported on its own rather than blended into one comfortable average: the very same AUROC ranking score, computed only for total strangers with no history. It's weaker there — around 0.70–0.75, still the identical yardstick, not a different or looser measure — and burying that inside one pooled number would quietly overstate how well the tool works for the patients we know least about.
Think of it like…
It's like a relay team of doctors instead of one all-knowing expert. The first makes a rough call, the next studies only where the first went wrong and corrects it, the next fixes what's still off — and hundreds of hand-offs later, the team catches subtle risk patterns no single doctor's rule of thumb ever would.

Step 4 of 6

What Else Could We Use

There were fancier options and simpler ones, but the model that best fit our messy patient data and gave an honest, act-on-it percentage won.

Quick recap. A "model" is just a recipe that turns a patient's info into a percentage guess — here, the chance they'll need intensive care (ICU-level care, the intense round-the-clock monitoring the sickest patients get). Different models are just different styles of recipe. The one we built — the project labels it M-03, which is simply this model's name — is a type called gradient-boosted trees. But it wasn't the only option on the table.

So how do you choose between recipes? Every option trades off four things: how accurate it is, how simple it is to explain, what shape of data it needs, and whether its output is an honest percentage you can actually act on. That last one matters a lot here, because M-03's number physically routes beds: above 40% risk, someone requests an ICU bed. (That 40% isn't a law of nature — it's a dial we set. Slide it lower and the alert fires more often, catching more true ICU cases but raising more false alarms; slide it higher and you get fewer false alarms but miss more real ones. 40% is the spot that balances those two.)

Let's meet the other candidates and see why each lost out. First, though, we need one shared yardstick to compare them fairly.

First, the yardstick: what AUROC means
To compare recipes fairly, we need one score everyone agrees on. It's called AUROC (short for "Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve" — a mouthful you can safely forget; what matters is the game it plays). The game: take one patient who truly needed the ICU and one who truly didn't, show the model both, and look at the risk score it gives each. Whichever one it scored higher is its guess for "this is the ICU patient." AUROC is just how often it guesses right across every such pair. 0.80 means right 80 times out of 100 — and that 0.80 is only a round teaching number here; our chosen model actually lands around 0.83. 0.50 is a pure coin flip (the model knows nothing useful); 1.00 is perfect every time. We use this particular yardstick because it doesn't depend on any single cutoff like the 40% line — it measures how well the model ranks patients across the whole range, so one number captures its overall skill. But a near-perfect score is a red flag, not a trophy. Quick vocabulary: patient data lives in a table with one row per patient and one column per fact (age, a lab value, and so on). "Leakage" is when some column secretly encodes the answer. Classic example: a column noting which ward the patient was later moved to — the value "ICU" only exists BECAUSE they ended up needing the ICU, so it's a fact from the future we'd never actually have at admission time. Feed that in and the model looks flawless while really just reading the answer off the page. Real health has genuine randomness, so "flawless" almost always means the model peeked. Suspiciously perfect means "go hunt for cheating," not "celebrate."
Logistic regression — the simple scorecard
This is a much simpler recipe: a scorecard where each factor adds or subtracts points (older age, +some points; a normal lab, −some). Then one fixed formula squashes the running total onto a 0–100% scale — a big positive total comes out near 100%, a big negative total near 0%, and a total around zero lands near 50%. That's literally how the points become a percentage. It's easy to explain and fast to run. But it scores a bit lower (about 0.79–0.82), and it has one built-in blind spot: it assumes each factor pushes the risk in the same direction by the same amount no matter what else is going on. Real medicine isn't always like that. Sometimes two factors only matter together — for example, a mildly high heart rate might be harmless in a healthy 25-year-old but alarming in a frail 80-year-old; it's the combination that's dangerous, not either fact on its own. A plain scorecard can't express "these two only count when they show up together." For a decision as serious as an ICU bed, we didn't want to trade away that ability just to keep things simple.
XGBoost — the close cousin we keep in reserve
To see why XGBoost is a cousin, it helps to unpack what our pick actually is. Start with a single decision tree: a flowchart of yes/no questions — "Is heart rate above 100? If yes, is age above 70? …" — that ends at a small risk estimate. One tree alone is crude. "Boosting" is the trick of building many trees in a row, where each new tree is trained specifically to fix the mistakes the trees so far are still making — the first makes rough guesses, the next focuses on the patients it got most wrong, and so on, so the committee's combined guess keeps improving. "Gradient" is just the name of the math that, at each step, works out which direction to nudge the next tree to shrink those mistakes fastest. Put it together and gradient-boosted trees = a committee of small flowcharts, each one patching the last one's errors. XGBoost is one specific software package that implements that exact idea — same recipe, different "engine" under the hood (different code, speed optimizations, and default settings). That's why it has its own name and shows up as a separate candidate even though the underlying method is the same. On our data it's about equally powerful, with no clear edge, so there was no strong reason to switch away from the version we already use. We keep it on the bench as a fallback in case we ever need more speed or muscle later.
NEWS2 — a trusted score that needs data we don't have yet
NEWS2 is a standard early-warning score doctors already use, added up from a fixed checklist of vital signs — breathing rate, oxygen level, temperature, blood pressure, pulse, and alertness. It's simple and widely trusted. The catch is that it needs that whole checklist, and in our records the complete set often isn't present at the exact admission moment we score from. It's not that vitals are never taken — it's that not all of them are entered into the system right away, and breathing rate in particular is famously under-recorded. NEWS2 with holes in its checklist isn't really NEWS2 anymore. On top of that, even when complete it's less accurate here (0.74–0.79). Missing pieces at the worst possible moment, plus lower accuracy, made it too shaky to route beds on its own.
Transformers — powerful, but built for the wrong shape of data
Transformers are the technology behind modern chatbots. They shine at making sense of sequences — a timeline of events in order, or a string of words in a sentence — where the ordering and the relationships between items carry the meaning. But M-03's input isn't a sequence. It's a single snapshot: one row of facts captured at the moment of admission, with no before-and-after. To be clear about what "overkill" means here: a transformer would still run, but it wouldn't merely waste extra computing power — it would most likely score worse. These models are data-hungry and only pay off when there's sequence structure to learn from; hand them a plain little snapshot and they tend to latch onto noise (overfit) and underperform the simpler options. Wrong tool, worse result.
Why gradient-boosted trees won
Two things sealed it, and notice that neither is simply "highest AUROC." First, they handle messy, incomplete patient tables gracefully. "Messy" here means, concretely: lots of blank cells (a lab never ordered, a vital never charted), values on wildly different scales (age in years sitting next to a lab in tiny decimal units), and the occasional odd outlier. A scorecard like logistic regression needs every blank filled in with a guess and every column rescaled by hand before it works well. Gradient-boosted trees don't: because each tree only asks yes/no questions, it can treat "this value is missing" as its own answer and send that patient down its own branch — no filling-in required — and because it only cares whether a value is above or below a split point, the raw scale of a column doesn't matter. That's the actual mechanism behind "graceful." It's the same reason our mortality model uses them. Second — and this is a genuinely different skill from AUROC — the percentage has to be honest in absolute terms, not just correctly ordered. These are two separate things a model can be good or bad at: AUROC only asks "did you rank the sicker patient higher?" (ordering), while we also need "when you say 70%, is it really about 70%?" (the value itself). A model can ace one and flunk the other — you can rank everyone perfectly while all your printed numbers read 20% too high. Because M-03's number literally trips a bed request at 40%, the number itself must be trustworthy. We fix this with calibration, which is a real adjustment step, not a hope: after training, we take the model's raw scores and check them against thousands of past patients — of everyone it scored "around 70%," how many truly needed the ICU? If the real answer was 60%, we bend all the 70% scores down to 60%, and repeat that across the whole range until the printed percentage matches reality. On accuracy the picture is honest and close: gradient-boosted trees land around 0.83, right at or just above the top of logistic regression's 0.79–0.82 band — a real but small edge. Accuracy alone wouldn't have decided it. What tipped it was that small edge plus the graceful handling of messy data plus a number we can calibrate to be genuinely actionable. No other option beat that whole mix.
Think of it like…
Picture you need to cross a small pond. A cargo ship (a transformer) is astonishingly powerful — but it's absurd overkill here, and it needs deep open water this pond doesn't have. A specialized racing kayak (NEWS2) is fast, but only when the water conditions are exactly right, and today they aren't. A simple raft (logistic regression) will get you across, just a little slower and clumsier. We picked the sturdy rowboat (gradient-boosted trees): not the flashiest, but it fits this exact pond, handles choppy messy water, and gets everyone across reliably. The lesson — match the boat to the water, not to how impressive the boat sounds.

Step 5 of 6

How We Know It Works

We test the tool three different ways, because for a life-and-death bed decision it has to be smart, honest, AND fair — not just one of those.

Quick reminder of the job. M-03 is the name of our prediction model. When a patient arrives, it guesses how likely they are to later need the ICU — the intensive care unit, the ward for the most critically ill patients, with the most staff and equipment per bed. That early guess lets the bed team reserve an ICU bed before a doctor formally confirms one is needed, buying precious hours.

Where does that guess even come from? Before it ever touched a real patient, M-03 studied thousands of past records where we already knew who ended up in the ICU, and it learned which early signs tended to come first. So "37%" isn't a hunch — it's that learned experience boiled down to one number. And it really is just one number per patient: there's no separate "ranking machine" and "percentage machine." (How the model is built is its own story; here we're only checking that it works.)

But a guess is only useful if it's a good guess. So how do we check? We don't lean on a single score — we use three different yardsticks, because here "good" honestly means three different things. We take that one number and ask three separate questions about it.

AUROC — can it sort the sick from the not-sick?
AUROC stands for "Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve" — a mouthful, and honestly not worth memorizing. What matters is the idea: it's a sorting test. Picture handing the model our old patient folders, where we already know who ended up in the ICU and who didn't. Take one patient who did and one who didn't and ask: did the model give the ICU patient the higher risk number? Now here's the key — we don't do this just once. We repeat that head-to-head pairing many thousands of times across all the folders, and AUROC is simply the fraction of times the model got the pair in the right order. That fraction lands between 0.50 and 1.00 — and notice this is its own 0-to-1 report-card scale for the whole model, NOT a percentage risk for one patient, so keep it separate in your head from the "37%" kind of number. Why is 0.50 the floor? Because a model with no real skill would still get each pair right about half the time out of pure luck — the same odds as calling a coin flip. So 0.50 means "no better than guessing" and 1.00 means "perfect every time." We want above 0.80: it puts the sicker person on top about 8 times out of 10. This is triage sense at work — triage being the everyday hospital job of deciding whose need is most urgent and who gets seen first. It's like a nurse who can reliably tell 'this person looks worse than that one' even without knowing the exact numbers.
Calibration — when it says 40%, does it mean it?
This is the big one, and it's a totally different question from sorting. Picture a weather forecaster whose ranking is fine — rainy days really do get higher numbers than dry ones — but who says '40% chance' for a batch of days where only 10% actually get rain. The order is right; the number is a lie. Calibration checks the literal value: of all the patients the model stamped with '40% risk,' did roughly 40 out of 100 truly end up needing the ICU? For M-03 this is non-negotiable, because the raw number itself triggers real action. '40%' only means something if it genuinely behaves like 4 in 10.
ECE — turning all those percentages into one calibration score
Quick clarification first, because this trips people up: calibration is the quality we care about (does '40%' really mean 40%?), and ECE — Expected Calibration Error — is simply the ruler we use to measure that quality. One is the goal, the other is the number that scores it; they are not two rival tests. Here's how the ruler works. It's a group check, not a person-by-person one — a single patient who got '40%' and then didn't need the ICU isn't 'wrong,' since 40% already means 'probably won't happen.' So instead we sort patients into bins by what the model said, like sorting mail by zip code: everyone in the 40-50% pile together, and so on. For each pile we compare what the model claimed on average (say, 44%) against what actually happened (say, only 22% needed the ICU). That gap is the pile's error. ECE is the average of all those gaps, weighted by pile size, so a 2,000-person pile counts more than a 20-person one. The result is a single number: 'on average, the stated percentage is off by about this much.'
A separate report card for brand-new patients
Some patients are first-timers — no past records in our system, so all M-03 has to go on is what we can measure right now: their age, their vitals (the basic body-function readings taken at the bedside — heart rate, blood pressure, temperature, breathing rate), and their early labs (results from blood and other samples sent to the lab soon after arrival). Why does having less to go on make the guess worse? Because the model makes its call by matching a patient against patterns it learned from the past. Fewer clues means fewer patterns to match, so it's forced to guess more — the same way you'd predict a stranger's next move less accurately than an old friend's. That's like grading a transfer student with no past transcript: not their fault, but you simply have less to judge by. So we grade this group on its own, and we genuinely expect a lower score here — around 0.70-0.75 on that same AUROC scale, instead of the 0.80+ we want overall. To be clear, that IS a real weakness, not a different passing grade: the tool is less sure about newcomers, full stop. But 0.70-0.75 is still useful (well above coin-flip 0.50), so we don't throw it out — we ship it with a warning label. It's more honest to tell the bed team 'trust this a bit less for brand-new patients' than to bury that gap inside one rosy overall average.
Who draws the 15% and 40% lines (and what's a step-down unit?)
People decide these cutoffs, not the model and not the math. Bed managers, ICU physicians and nursing leaders set them: under 15% go to a regular ward, 15-40% to a step-down unit, over 40% reserve an ICU bed. Where you draw those lines is a risk-tolerance judgment — meaning, which kind of mistake are you more willing to live with? Set the bar too low and you get false alarms: patients flagged high-risk who never actually needed the ICU (wasted beds and effort). Set it too high and you get missed patients: people waved through as low-risk who did crash and need the ICU (the dangerous mistake). No single cutoff avoids both, so humans pick the trade-off they can defend. As for a step-down unit, think of a ladder. A regular ward is like a hotel room where a nurse pops in every few hours. A step-down unit (also called intermediate care) means closer watching and extra monitors, but no breathing machine. The ICU is the top rung: one nurse per one or two patients, and machines that can breathe for you. And remember — M-03 always prints an exact number like '37%.' The three buckets are just a human-made translation layer bolted on top, like a thermostat that reads 68.3 degrees but simply displays 'comfortable.'
Why three checks and not just one
Because each one catches a different way the tool could quietly fail. AUROC catches 'does it even know who's sicker?' Calibration catches 'is the printed percentage trustworthy enough to act on directly?' The no-history split catches 'is it secretly much worse for a whole group of real people?' A model could ace the sorting test and still print wildly wrong percentages — fine if all you wanted was an internal ranking, disastrous when the number itself books a bed. Checking all three is how we make sure the tool isn't just clever, but honest and fair enough to safely hand real hospital decisions to.
Think of it like…
Think of testing a kitchen thermometer. One test asks: does it show the soup as hotter than the fridge? (Just the order — you don't care about the exact degrees.) A second test asks: when it reads 212 degrees, is the water actually boiling? (Now the exact number matters.) M-03 is that thermometer. AUROC is the first test, calibration is the second — the same single reading, two very different questions.

Step 1 of 6

The Big Picture

M-04 estimates the odds a patient will land back in the hospital within 30 days — on the very day they're admitted — so the care team can act early instead of finding out too late.

Picture a nurse sending a patient home. In the back of their mind is one nagging question: will this person actually be okay, or will they be back in the ER in a couple of weeks? M-04 is a computer program that makes an educated guess at exactly that, for every patient, the moment they're admitted (checked in). It estimates the chance they'll be readmitted — come back to the hospital — within 30 days of going home. (The name is nothing fancy: "M" just stands for model, and it happens to be the fourth one the team built. It tells you nothing about what the tool does, so don't read anything into it.)

Here's the key thing: it doesn't answer "yes" or "no." It answers with a percentage — something like "22% chance this patient comes back." It's a forecast, not a promise. (Throughout this page, "odds," "chance," and "probability" all mean the same everyday thing: how likely something is, from 0% to 100%. There's no hidden statistical trick behind the different words.)

Why bother? Because hospitals lose sight of patients the second they walk out the door. Some heal just fine. Others run out of medicine, have no one to check on them, and end up back sicker than before. M-04's whole job is to spot the high-risk people on day one, while there's still time to do something. And 30 days isn't a random cutoff: a return within one month is the yardstick the whole healthcare system uses to judge whether a recovery plan held together, and hospitals are even scored — and financially penalized — on it.

It learned from real patients — it isn't a formula someone typed up
M-04 isn't a checklist a person sat down and wrote by hand. It's machine learning: it was shown the records of hundreds of thousands of past patients — who bounced back and who didn't — and it taught itself the patterns that separate the two. That also answers the fair question, 'how do we know it's any good?' Its percentages are calibrated, which means they've been checked against what actually happened: out of everyone it labels '22%,' roughly 22 in 100 really do come back. So the odds are honest — even though no forecast can ever be certain about one single person.
What it looks at — and what it's forbidden to peek at
The 'facts' it reads are things already known at admission: the patient's age, why they were admitted, what medications they're on, their earliest lab results, and how many times they've been hospitalized before. What it must NOT touch is the discharge paperwork — the summary a doctor writes at the END of the stay describing how everything actually turned out. Using that would be like grading a test with the answer key stapled to the back: unfair, and impossible in real life anyway, because on day one that paperwork doesn't even exist yet.
A forecast, not a verdict
The output is a probability — a chance, like a rain forecast — not a flat 'they will' or 'they won't.' Take that 22% patient: 22 out of 100 similar patients come back, which means the other 78 out of 100 stay home. So a 22% score actually says the person is far more likely to be fine than to return. The number is the odds for the group, not a sentence handed to that one individual.
It's a day-one snapshot
The score is calculated once, right at admission — and that's the entire point. The earlier the warning, the more time the team has to change the outcome. Think of it as a first read that hands staff a heads-up on day one, not a live number that ticks up and down through the whole stay. From there, human staff take over and decide what to do with it.
Two things come out: a number and a tier
First a probability (like 22%). Then that number gets sorted into a labeled bucket called a risk tier — Low, Medium, High, or Very High. The cutoffs between tiers (8%, 18%, 30%) aren't magic numbers plucked from the air; clinicians chose them to strike a balance — low enough to catch the patients who genuinely need help, high enough that staff aren't buried in false alarms. The tier is what turns a raw percentage into a clear instruction for the team.
Crossing a line sets off real action — but a person can still say no
The tier isn't just a score sitting in a database. Reaching 'High' automatically books two things. A social worker — hospital staff who handle the non-medical side of getting well, like arranging rides home, affordable medications, and follow-up appointments. And home-care support — a nurse or aide who checks on the patient at their house after discharge. It fires automatically so nobody slips through on a busy shift, like a smoke detector that doesn't just beep but actually calls the fire department. That said, it's a starting point, not a locked order: if staff know a particular patient doesn't need it, they can cancel the referral.
Think of it like…
Think of M-04 like a weather forecast. When a forecaster says "22% chance of rain," they aren't wrong if the day stays dry — a 22% day is supposed to be dry most of the time. M-04 works the same way: it hands you the odds for each patient, not a guarantee about any single one.

Step 2 of 6

What Goes In, What Comes Out

M-04 takes only what's known the day a patient arrives and turns it into a clear risk score, the reasons behind it, and — for the patients who need it most — an automatic next step.

Think of M-04 as a machine with one slot in and one slot out. Really, it's a computer program — a prediction model the hospital runs quietly in the background. (The "M-04" name is just an internal label, like the model number stamped on an appliance; it doesn't stand for anything you need to decode.) Into the slot goes a snapshot of the patient, taken the very moment they walk through the hospital door. Out the other side comes an answer to a single question: how likely is this person to be back in the hospital within 30 days of going home?

Here's the one rule that shapes everything. M-04 is only allowed to look at facts that already exist on day one. Nothing from later in the stay. If it peeked at the doctor's final wrap-up notes, it would basically be reading the ending of the story to predict the story, and on a real day one nobody has that ending yet. That kind of sneaky peeking is called leakage, and M-04 is built to avoid it.

And what comes out isn't a flat yes or no. It's a percentage, a simple risk label (later on we'll also call it a risk tier — same thing, just the bucket the percentage falls into), a short list of plain-English reasons, and, for the riskiest patients, an automatic nudge to the care team. Let's walk through both sides of the machine.

What goes in: a day-one snapshot
The inputs are all things the hospital already knows the moment a patient arrives, with no waiting and no guessing. Age and sex. How many times this person has been admitted before. What conditions showed up in their past visits. How many medicines they're on. How they came in — the hospital actually logs several admission types (emergency, planned/elective, a transfer from another facility, and so on), but the signal M-04 leans on most is simply emergency versus planned. And one ready-made score called LACE. The table below lays each one out.
'Polypharmacy' is just a fancy word for 'a lot of pills'
You'll hear this term a lot, so let's demystify it. Polypharmacy simply means someone is taking many different medicines at once. Think of it as a warning light on a dashboard. A person juggling 12 pill bottles has far more room for a mix-up, a bad drug interaction, or a health crisis than someone taking one or two. M-04 mostly cares about the count — specifically, the count of medicines the patient is actively on when they arrive, not every drug they've ever been prescribed in the past.
LACE, spelled out: one clue, not the whole verdict
LACE is a small, well-tested scorecard doctors already use on their own. Its name is an acronym for the four things it rolls into a single number: **L**ength of stay, **A**cuity (how sick and urgent the case looks at the moment of arrival — a reading taken once at check-in, so it stays inside the day-one rule and isn't updated later), **C**omorbidity (how many other ongoing illnesses the patient has), and **E**mergency-room visits (recent trips to the ER). You'll notice some of these overlap with the standalone inputs — past illnesses and hospital history appear both on their own and again folded inside LACE. That double-listing is deliberate, not a mistake: M-04 can weigh a fact directly and also as part of the LACE bundle, and it works out how much each version should count. One thing to keep straight: an ER visit isn't the same as an admission — an ER trip can end with the patient going home, while an admission means they were kept in a hospital bed — so M-04 counts those two separately. It doesn't throw LACE away; it folds that number in as one ingredient among many, then adds more sensors on top.
Whose history? This exact person's — and first-timers still work
When M-04 checks 'past visits,' it means this same patient's own records, not a stranger's and not 'people like them.' It's like a teacher pulling up your report cards, not a classmate's. And by 'conditions' it means any diagnosis a doctor recorded on those past visits — from big ongoing ones like heart failure down to smaller entries — drawn from the standard coded list hospitals keep, not only the serious ones. And if it's someone's very first-ever hospital stay? The model doesn't freeze up. It reads 'past visits' as zero and leans harder on the other clues — age, meds, how they came in, and LACE. Much like judging a brand-new hire on their resume and interview instead of a track record they don't have yet.
Why day-one only? So it can't cheat
Imagine giving a student a test but slipping them the answer key first. They'd ace it, and you'd learn nothing about what they actually know. Letting M-04 see late-in-the-stay information would be the same trick. It would look brilliant in testing and be useless in real life, because on a real day one that information doesn't exist yet. So M-04 is deliberately kept a little 'blind,' which is exactly what makes its predictions trustworthy — and because of that rule, the score is locked in from day-one facts and never quietly rewritten with things learned later in the stay.
What comes out: a percentage, a tier, and — when needed — an action
First, a probability — a number like 0.22, meaning a 22% chance. It's a forecast, not a promise. Then that number gets sorted into a plain bucket so nobody has to squint at decimals: Low (under 8%), Medium (8 to 18%), High (18 to 30%), or Very High (over 30%) — four tiers that line up end to end, with no gaps and no overlaps. Every patient gets a tier, but the automatic action only kicks in at High and above: the system opens a social-work referral (looping in a hospital social worker, whose job is lining up practical support — rides to follow-ups, help affording medicines, arranging help at home) and sets up a home-care check (a nurse or aide checking in on the patient once they're home, in person or by a scheduled follow-up). For Low and Medium patients, the 'next step' is simply routine discharge — no special referral; the tier just stays there as information for the team. And because the score is fixed on day one, that referral can be opened early in the stay, giving the team the whole stay to act before the patient leaves.
It also tells you WHY, in plain words
This is the part clinicians love. Instead of a lonely number, M-04 lists the top reasons behind that specific patient's score, something like '11 active medicines, 2 recent ER visits, active heart failure.' How does it pick the 'top' ones? It measures how much each input pushed that particular patient's number up, then names the few that pushed hardest — so the reasons are an actual calculation, not a guess. It's the difference between a mechanic saying 'your car needs work' and pointing at the three worn parts. Now the social worker doesn't just know someone is risky. They know why, and what to tackle first.
Think of it like…
M-04 is like a weather station. You feed it readings — temperature, wind, pressure — and it doesn't shout "it will rain!" It says "30% chance of rain today." In the same spirit, M-04 takes in a patient's day-one facts and hands back "22% chance of coming back within a month" — a calm, honest odds estimate you can actually plan around, not a flat guarantee. And here's why you can trust that number: just as a good forecast is checked against how often it really rains, M-04's percentages have been tested against what actually happened to past patients, so a 22% really does mean that about 22 out of 100 similar people came back — a measured odds, not a wild guess.

Step 3 of 6

Why We Chose This Model

We picked a model that can spot sudden jumps in risk a straight line would miss — and then forced it to explain every call.

On the day a patient is admitted, M-04 — our in-house name for this readmission-risk model, the fourth build in a numbered series — asks one question: if we send this person home, what's the chance they'll be back in the hospital within 30 days? It turns a few basic facts — age, past hospital stays, how many daily medicines they take, past illnesses — into a percentage, then sorts that percentage into a risk tier. Roughly: under 10% is Low, 10–25% is Medium, 25–50% is High, and above 50% is Very High. If someone lands in "High" or "Very High," the hospital lines up a social worker and home-care help before that patient even leaves.

That sounds simple. But the model doing the guessing was a real decision. The obvious choice is the simplest possible tool — and we deliberately did not use it.

The short version: real patient risk doesn't rise in a smooth, straight line. It has tipping points. We needed a model that could find them, catch the risky people a simpler tool smooths over, and still tell us why it flagged someone. Here's the reasoning, step by step.

The simplest tool draws a straight line
The easiest option is called logistic regression — picture it as a straight-line model, or a ruler. It assumes every extra unit of something adds the same fixed amount of risk, no matter where you start. So going from 4 medicines to 5 counts exactly the same as going from 14 to 15. Clean and easy to trust — but that sameness is also its weakness.
But real risk has tipping points
Patient risk doesn't behave like a ruler. It behaves more like a shelf you keep stacking books on: nothing much happens for a while, then past a certain point it suddenly cracks. Going from 14 medicines to 15 — a body already juggling a dangerous drug cocktail — can jump the risk sharply, while 4 to 5 barely moves it. A straight line can't tell those two '+1' steps apart. It smears the danger out evenly and misses the snap.
The data hides two more traps
First, imbalance: only about 15 in 100 patients actually come back, so a lazy model could just guess 'nobody returns' and look 85% 'accurate' while helping no one. Second, leakage: the richest document about a stay — the doctor's discharge summary — is written after the whole stay is over, so leaning on it would be like reading the answer key before the test. We're only allowed to use what's known on day one. That second trap is also why we set aside text-reading models. You might reasonably ask: couldn't we just feed a text model the notes written at admission instead — same day-one rule as everything else? The catch is that at admission the written notes are thin and patchy, and the one genuinely rich document (the discharge summary) is off-limits. A note-reading model would be starved of exactly the text it's good at, so we leaned on the structured day-one facts instead.
Our choice: a committee that grades itself
So we chose gradient-boosted decision trees. Don't let the name scare you. A 'decision tree' is just a chain of yes/no questions that branches — 'On more than 10 medicines? Yes goes one way, No the other, then each side asks its next question' — and because every answer splits into two branches, the whole thing fans out into the shape of a tree. That's all the word 'tree' means here. 'Gradient-boosted' means we don't build one tree; we build a long line of them, like a committee where each member is one small tree. Here's the trick that makes them a committee and not just a crowd. We train on past patients — people already in our records whose real 30-day outcome we know. The first tree makes its guesses. For each patient we then measure the leftover gap: the number the committee has guessed so far minus what actually happened. ('What actually happened' is written as a plain number — 1 if they came back, 0 if they didn't — so a 70% guess for someone who did return leaves a gap of 0.30.) The next tree isn't asked to predict readmission from scratch. Its only job is to predict that leftover gap — to clean up exactly where the committee is currently wrong — and we add its answer onto the running total. Each new member is a specialist in fixing the previous total's misses (that's the same 'committee member,' just doing repair work). Stack enough of them and the committee gets sharp. Because it's built from branching yes/no questions, it naturally catches the 'past 10 medicines' snap that a straight line glosses over.
We locked in a common-sense rule
A raw tree committee can still do odd things, so we bolted on a rule called a monotonic constraint. In plain terms: more medicines, more past illnesses, or more past hospital stays can only ever push the risk up — never down. It stops the model from deciding, on the strength of some quirk in the training data, that a patient on 20 drugs is somehow safer than one on 5. Think of a scale that's only allowed to tip one way as you pile on weight.
We made it explain itself with SHAP
We also require the model to show its work, using SHAP — short for SHapley Additive exPlanations. The idea is borrowed from Lloyd Shapley, an economist who won the 2012 Nobel Prize for a piece of game theory that answers this question: when a team wins a prize together, how do you fairly split the credit among players who each contributed differently? SHAP treats a patient's facts as the 'players' and the risk score as the 'prize.' For each patient, it works out how much each fact contributed by re-running the already-trained model with and without that fact switched on — across many combinations of the other facts — and averaging how much the score moves. (Important: it never retrains the model. It just re-tests the finished model with different facts hidden or shown.) The result is an itemized receipt: 'high because of 11 active medicines, 2 recent ER visits, active heart failure.' It's not a story invented afterward — it's the model's real math. One honest caveat: SHAP faithfully reports whatever the model actually used, so if the model leaned on something clinically silly, SHAP would honestly show the silly thing too. What actually guards against that is upstream: we only ever let the model see a curated list of clinically sensible facts (nothing like a patient's zip code gets in), we pin down its logic with the monotonic rule above, and a clinician reads these receipts to sanity-check them. The performance checks below can't by themselves tell 'silly' from 'sensible' — that job is done by controlling what goes in and reviewing what comes out.
How we know it's actually better
We check three separate things, and it helps to keep them apart. First — ranking — measured by a score called AUROC. Read it as a ranking contest, not a 'percent correct.' Hand the model two patients from our past records, one who did bounce back and one who didn't, and ask which is riskier; AUROC is how often it picks the right one. This is fairer than plain accuracy, because with only 15% readmitted, guessing 'nobody' would score a useless 85%. The traditional tools — the straight-line model plus two well-known pen-and-paper point systems, LACE and HOSPITAL — land around 68–72%; our tree committee reaches 75–78%. (LACE and HOSPITAL are named for the ingredients they add up: LACE = Length of stay, Acuity of the admission, Comorbidities, Emergency visits; HOSPITAL is a similar acronym built from lab values and admission details. Both are hand-fillable scorecards.) Second, and separately — calibration: when the model says '22% risk,' do about 22 out of 100 such patients really come back? A model can rank people correctly yet still be systematically over- or under-confident, so this is its own distinct test. Third, again separate — precision at the top tier: of the patients we flag 'Very High,' how many truly return? This is the 'don't cry wolf' check. If most Very-High alarms turn out false, staff stop trusting the flag and it becomes useless.
Think of it like…
Picture stacking books on a cheap shelf. A straight-line model expects it to sag a little more with each book — evenly and predictably. But real shelves don't work that way. They hold, hold, hold, then suddenly crack. Patient risk is the same: steady for a while, then a sharp jump once you cross a threshold. We chose a model that can find that cracking point, instead of one that pretends the shelf sags smoothly.

Step 4 of 6

What Else Could We Use

The team compared four possible models for M-04 — their 30-day readmission predictor — and picked gradient-boosted trees, because it was the only one smart enough to catch real risk, honest enough not to cheat, and clear enough for a human to act on.

M-04 is just the team's internal name for one model on their roster — read it as "Model #4." ("The team" means the hospital's data scientists working hand-in-hand with its doctors — you'll see why the doctors matter shortly.) Its one job: the moment a patient is admitted, guess how likely they are to bounce back to the hospital within 30 days of going home. If the risk is high, the team can line up extra help — like a social worker — before the patient even leaves.

Before settling on one model, they looked at four different ways to make that guess. Think of each one as a candidate "smoke detector" for readmission risk. Three of them lost. Here is each candidate, in plain words, and why the winner won.

The short version: the simple models were too rigid, the powerful note-reading model cheated, and the winner threaded the needle. Let's walk through them one at a time.

Option 1: Logistic regression — the simple scorecard
First, a word we'll lean on: a risk factor is just any detail about the patient that nudges their risk up or down — their age, how many times they've been hospitalized before, how many medicines they take. Logistic regression is like a report card where every risk factor gets a fixed number of points that you add up: +2 for age, +3 per past hospital stay, +1 per medicine. Notice the word 'fixed': if every extra medicine always adds exactly the same 1 point, then plot risk against number-of-medicines and you get a perfectly straight line — a steady, even climb. That's the whole idea of logistic regression: one straight-line relationship between each factor and risk. The trouble is that real risk doesn't climb in a straight line. Going from 4 medicines to 5 barely matters, but going from 9 to 10 might be a real warning sign — like a light switch that flips on past a certain point, not a dimmer that rises evenly. A straight-line scorecard can't tell those two jumps apart. It's simpler and easy to explain, but noticeably less accurate. How do we know it's less accurate? Every model here is graded on real past patients whose outcome is already recorded — people discharged years ago, where the files already say who did and didn't come back within 30 days. We let the model guess, then check its guesses against what actually happened. The grading measure is called AUROC (Area Under the ROC Curve). Concretely: pick two real patients — one who came back, one who didn't — have the model score each, and check whether it gave the one who came back the higher score. AUROC is simply the share of all such pairs it gets right. 1.0 means it wins every pairing (a perfect ranker); 0.5 means it's guessing (a coin flip). Logistic regression lands around 0.68–0.72.
Option 2: LACE and HOSPITAL scores — the hand-built checklists
These are older, already-published paper checklists — like a symptom sheet a nurse fills in by hand, where each answer adds points. LACE stands for Length of stay, Acuity, Comorbidities, and Emergency-room visits. (Acuity means how sick or urgent the patient was on the way in — rushed through the ER, say, versus a calm scheduled admission. A comorbidity is just another disease the patient already has, like diabetes on top of the heart problem they came in for.) HOSPITAL is a second checklist whose letters are a loose mnemonic for the things it tallies: low Hemoglobin (blood count) at discharge, discharge from an Oncology (cancer) ward, low Sodium level, whether a Procedure was done this stay, an urgent Index (this) admission, how many Times they were admitted in the past year, and a long Length of stay. Both were designed by doctors from expert judgment, not learned from data — and both were built decades ago on other hospitals' patients, so they don't adapt to what MIMIC's specific patients actually look like. Their accuracy is similar to logistic regression (about 0.68–0.72). Still, the team kept them — not as the main model, but as a sanity check: if the fancy model ever disagrees wildly with a trusted old checklist, that's a signal something might be wrong.
Option 3: Transformer — the model that reads the notes (but cheats)
A transformer is a text-reading AI — the same family of technology behind chatbots. Mechanically, it scans a document and, for every word, weighs how strongly each other word should sway its verdict, juggling millions of internal numbers to do it. The plan was to have one read the discharge summary — the doctor's write-up of everything that happened during the stay — and predict readmission from it. Two problems sank it. The dealbreaker is leakage: using information the model wouldn't actually have at prediction time. The discharge summary is written after the patient's story is basically over, and it may even say things like 'discharged with a plan for close follow-up due to high readmission risk.' Using it is like taking a test with the answer key glued to the back page — it looks brilliant, but it's just reading the answer. Since M-04 has to work the day the patient is admitted, before that summary even exists, this approach isn't usable here at all, no matter how accurate it looks. The second problem — and the reason the table below calls it hard to explain — is that a transformer's 'reasoning' is exactly that tangle of millions of weighted numbers. When it spits out a risk, there's no tidy list of reasons to hand a nurse, just the tangle. A scorecard, by contrast, lets you literally point to the points.
The winner: gradient-boosted decision trees, with add-ons
This model works like a long chain of yes/no questions — a machine version of 20 Questions: 'More than 8 medicines? yes → more than 2 ER visits this year? yes → active heart failure? yes → high risk.' Because it asks questions in sequence, it naturally catches the sudden jumps in risk that a straight-line scorecard misses. Two things were bolted on, and they do very different jobs. The first is a real guardrail: a monotonic constraint — a rule the team forces on the model so that, for a chosen factor, risk may only rise or stay flat as that factor grows, never fall (full details in the follow-up just below). The second is not a guardrail at all — it doesn't stop or change anything the model does; it only explains, after the fact. It's called SHAP (short for SHapley Additive exPlanations), and it works like an itemized receipt for each prediction: instead of just '72% risk,' it prints the top reasons — '11 active medicines, 2 recent ER visits, active heart failure.' That receipt is what lets a social worker trust and act on the number instead of treating it as a mystery. Best accuracy (0.75–0.78 AUROC) and still explainable — which is why it won.
Follow-up: how do all those question-chains become one number?
Imagine a team of guessers trying to guess someone's weight, where each new guesser only sees how far off the last guess was. Guesser #1 says '170 lbs'; the real answer is 190, so they're off by 20. That leftover 20 — the size and direction of the miss — is what the next guesser aims at. (That leftover error is exactly what the 'gradient' in gradient-boosted trees refers to: 'gradient' is just the math word for 'which way, and how far, the last guess was off,' and each new tree is trained to chase it.) So Guesser #2's only job is to guess that leftover 20 — they say '+15,' bringing the running total to 185. Guesser #3 guesses the remaining 5, says '+4,' and now we're at 189. Closer and closer. That's exactly what the chain of trees does, except each tree nudges a risk score instead of a weight, and each one outputs only a tiny correction like '+0.3' or '-0.1,' never a final answer. Nobody adds these up by hand. The computer runs the patient through every tree and sums all the little corrections into one running total. But that total is just a raw number on some open-ended scale — it isn't a percentage yet. So one last fixed step converts it: an S-shaped formula (the very same 'squeeze' that logistic regression uses) that takes any number, however large or small, and gently maps it onto the 0–100% band — very negative totals land near 0%, very positive ones near 100%, middling ones in between. Our weight-guessers didn't need this step because '189 lbs' was already a real-world answer; a risk model needs it because 'chance of coming back' has to come out as a percentage. The whole pipeline — every tree plus that final squeeze — is baked into the trained model and runs the instant you feed in a new patient.
Follow-up: what is a 'monotonic constraint,' and can it backfire?
This one is written in by a human — the computer doesn't discover it on its own. During setup, the team tells the software: for these specific inputs — number of medicines, number of past stays, number of comorbidities — you may only learn a pattern where risk goes up or stays flat as the number rises, never down. It's like putting a one-way turnstile on certain doors in the question chain: the model can decide a factor barely matters, but it's physically blocked from building a branch where 'more medicines' leads to lower risk. And yes, it can backfire. It's only safe for factors where medical sense says the relationship really is one-directional. Blood pressure, for example, is U-shaped — both very low and very high are dangerous, while the middle is fine. Force a 'more is worse' rule onto blood pressure and you'd handcuff the model into learning something medically false, making it less accurate. So which factors get the rule isn't a default and isn't guesswork: the data scientists decide it together with the hospital's doctors, factor by factor, leaning on medical knowledge of which relationships are genuinely one-way. (That's the main place a doctor's judgment steers this model.)
Follow-up: how does a percentage become an actual decision?
For each patient, the model's raw output is just one number — a probability, like '72% chance of readmission in 30 days.' On its own, that number doesn't say 'send a social worker.' Someone has to draw a line. Think of a fever thermometer: 100.9°F is just a number, but doctors separately agreed that 100.4°F and up counts as a fever worth acting on. Same idea — the hospital picks a cutoff. How? Not by gut feel: they take the model's scores on those same past patients (whose real outcomes are known), try many possible cutoffs, and for each one lay out the trade-off — how many true bounce-backs it catches versus how many false alarms it raises. Set the line too low and you catch nearly every bounce-back but bury the social workers in false alarms; set it too high and you miss real at-risk patients. The data scientists show that trade-off, and hospital staff make the final call on something practical: how many social workers they actually have. This is also where the winner's higher accuracy quietly pays off — a more accurate model (higher AUROC) separates the will-come-backs from the won'ts more cleanly, so at whatever cutoff you pick it catches the same real cases with fewer false alarms than the shakier models would (that's the 'calm about false alarms' part of the smoke-detector picture below). Rather than one line, M-04 sorts patients into tiers so the response scales with the risk: Low means routine discharge with no extra action; Medium triggers a follow-up phone call; High puts the patient on a social worker's list for a chart review and outreach; and Very High books an in-person social-work visit before the patient leaves. And this is where SHAP's receipt earns its keep — the worker sees why the number is high and can sanity-check it, rather than blindly trusting a mystery percentage.
Think of it like…
M-04 is like choosing a smoke detector for a house. A cheap one (the simple scorecard or checklist) is easy to understand but misses fires that build slowly. A super-sensitive lab sensor that reads the fire report written after the fire (the note-reading transformer) is useless for prevention — the danger is already over. What you actually want is the detector that reliably catches real smoke, doesn't scream every time you make toast, and tells you which room the smoke is in so you know where to run. That's the gradient-boosted-trees model with SHAP: accurate, calm about false alarms, and clear about why it's beeping.

Step 5 of 6

How We Know It Works

A good risk model has to do two things — sort patients by risk, and put honest, actionable numbers on them — so we run three separate tests, because there are three different ways it can fail.

How do you decide whether to trust M-04 — the name is nothing fancy, just our fourth model? Its entire job is answering one question: "will this patient end up back in the hospital within 30 days of going home?" That coming-back-within-30-days event is what we call a readmission, and for every patient the model prints a single number: their risk of one, somewhere from 0% to 100%.

That number does two things, so the model really has two jobs. First, it sorts patients from safest to riskiest — a ranking. Second, the percentages get grouped into four action tiers — Low, Medium, High, and Very High (roughly under 10%, 10–30%, 30–60%, and 60%+, with the exact lines drawn by our team, not the model) — and anyone landing in Very High automatically gets a social worker and a home-care visit lined up. That automatic hand-off is what we'll call a referral. So the model has to (a) rank people correctly AND (b) hand a busy human a number honest enough to act on without double-checking it.

You wouldn't lean on a brand-new nurse's gut after one good shift — you'd check their track record, and in more than one way. We check M-04 three ways, and the three map neatly onto its two jobs: Test 1 checks the ranking; Tests 2 and 3 check whether the number and the action it triggers can be trusted. Each test is built to catch a different kind of mistake.

Test 1 — Can it even tell risky from safe? (AUROC)
The first thing to check is whether the model gets the ordering right. AUROC stands for "Area Under the ROC Curve." Here's the curve part, briefly: as you slide the risk cutoff from strict to loose, the model catches more true readmissions but also raises more false alarms — plot that trade-off (true catches versus false alarms) and you get the ROC curve. The one number we actually use, the area under that curve, turns out to answer a simple question: pick a patient who really did come back and one who didn't — does the model give the returner the higher risk score? A score of 0.50 means pure guessing, like a coin flip; 1.00 means it never gets a pair backwards. Our target is 0.75–0.78 — so about 3 out of 4 pairs ranked correctly. Why not aim for 0.95? Because readmission is genuinely hard to predict: a lot of what sends someone back (a fall at home, a missed ride to the pharmacy) was never written in any chart. Published readmission models land right around 0.75, so that range is "good for this problem," not a consolation prize. If a model can't clear it, nothing else matters — it's just noise in a lab coat.
How that 0.75 is actually calculated (no dice involved)
You might picture someone pulling random pairs of patients out of a hat over and over. Nice image, but that's not what happens — the computer compares every possible pairing at once. Here's a tiny made-up example just to show the math (the real numbers are far bigger): say 50 patients came back and 50 didn't. Every returner is matched against every non-returner, which is 50 × 50 = 2,500 head-to-head matchups. AUROC is simply the fraction of all those matchups the model got right. Because it checks every pair with one quick formula, there's nothing random in the final answer — run it again on the same data and you get the exact same 0.75 every time.
Test 2 — When it says 20%, does it mean 20%? (Calibration / ECE)
Ranking isn't enough, because M-04 doesn't just sort patients — it hands a human an actual percentage, and the four tiers are built on those percentages. So we check calibration: are the numbers honest? Think of a weather forecaster who says "70% chance of rain" but it only rains on 40% of those days — exciting, but dishonest. Calibration asks the same of our model: of all the patients it labeled "20% risk," did roughly 20% actually come back? ECE (Expected Calibration Error) boils that down to one number — the average gap between the stated percentages and reality. ("Expected" isn't a special code word here; it's just the statistics term for "average.") Smaller is better; zero is perfect. This matters because the tiers ride entirely on these numbers: if the model's "30%" patients are really more like 60%, the Very High cutoff catches the wrong people and referrals fire for them.
But one person is never "20% readmitted" — so where does that number come from?
You spotted something real: a single person either comes back or doesn't — there's no 20%-readmitted version of one human. So that 20% is never a fact about one person's future; it's a statement about a crowd of people who look like them. Back to the forecaster: "70% chance" really means "of every past day with this same wind, pressure, and clouds, it rained on 70 out of 100." The model does the same. It looks at a patient's traits — medicine count, recent ER visits, active conditions, age — finds past patients with similar numbers, sees that about 20 in 100 of them were readmitted, and stamps that crowd's track record onto the new person. It's also how we check calibration after the fact: we gather everyone scored "around 20%" and see what fraction of that whole bucket actually came back.
How ECE is actually calculated (a quick worked example)
Just like AUROC, ECE has a concrete recipe, so here it is. Sort every patient into buckets by their stated risk — a "0–10%" bucket, a "10–20%" bucket, and so on up. For each bucket, compare what the model promised against what really happened: say the "20%" bucket holds 200 patients and 35% of them actually came back — that bucket is off by 15 points. Do that for every bucket, then take the average of those gaps, weighted by how many patients sit in each (a crowded bucket that's wrong hurts the score more than a nearly empty one). That weighted average is the ECE. A model with an ECE of about 0.03 is off by roughly 3 points on average — close enough to trust the tiers.
Test 3 — When it screams "fire," is there usually a fire? (Precision at the top)
This last test zooms in on just the alarm-triggering group — the Very High patients who get that social-worker-and-home-care referral. Precision at the top asks: of everyone flagged Very High, how many really do get readmitted? Think of a smoke detector that shrieks at burnt toast every morning — eventually everyone tunes it out, including during a real fire. If the Very High bucket is mostly false alarms, we burn scarce social-work hours and staff stop trusting the flags. This is the metric closest to real-world cost: AUROC and calibration ask whether the model is statistically sound, but precision at the top asks whether the specific action we take actually pays off often enough.
Who draws the "Very High" line — and why you can't cheat with it
The model does not decide where "Very High" starts — a person does. The model just prints a percentage for everyone (5%, 20%, 62%). Where to put the cutoff is a separate human choice, and it's driven by staffing: we have enough social workers for only about the top 8% of patients each week. We take that 8% quota and read off the risk percentage it corresponds to in the current data — which this season happens to land around 60%. So the two ways you might hear the cutoff described — "top 8% each week" and "60% and up" — aren't rival rules that could disagree; the staffing quota is the real driver, and the 60% is just what that quota translates to right now. And yes, sliding that line changes the precision number without the model changing at all. Raise the bar (say 80%+) and precision usually climbs, because you keep only the surest cases — but you miss more real readmissions. Lower it (40%+) and precision drops as shakier cases sweep in, but you catch more true returns. That tug-of-war — being right when you flag (precision) versus catching everyone you should (recall) — is normal. The honest rule: pick the threshold first, from a real constraint, then look at precision — never the other way around.
Why we need all three, together
Each test guards against a failure the other two would miss. A model can ace one and quietly flunk another: it might rank patients beautifully (great AUROC) while being wildly overconfident about the exact percentages (bad calibration), or have honest percentages on average while its top alarm bucket is still mostly noise (bad precision at the top). Remember the two jobs from the start — rank correctly, and hand out a trustworthy number-and-action. Test 1 vouches for the first job; Tests 2 and 3 together vouch for the second. That's why there are two jobs but three tests, and why no single test can vouch for the whole model on its own.
Why gradient-boosted trees — and why we still trust the reasons
One last thing behind the numbers: the kind of model we chose. We started with logistic regression — a "straight-line" model (same thing, two names). Straight-line means it assumes every extra medicine adds the same fixed bump of risk, so the step from 4 to 5 medicines counts exactly as much as the step from 14 to 15. But real risk doesn't rise in even steps: it can stay flat for a while, then jump sharply once you cross a danger zone (10+ drugs, a 3rd ER visit). That straight-line model topped out around 0.68–0.72 AUROC. So we switched to gradient-boosted trees. A "tree" is just a stack of yes/no questions ("more than 10 medicines? been to the ER 3+ times? heart failure active?"), and each answer nudges the patient's score up or down a little; add up all the nudges across hundreds of these small trees and convert the total into a 0–100% risk. Because trees can bend right at those danger-zone thresholds instead of drawing one straight line, they capture the jumps — which is why they beat logistic regression. Finally, so a flag isn't just "62%, trust me," we run a tool called SHAP. For each patient, SHAP adds up how much each individual fact pushed the score up or down, then hands back a plain-English list — "11 active medicines, 2 recent ER visits, active heart failure" — so the social worker sees exactly why this person surfaced.
Think of it like…
Judging M-04 is like judging a new weather forecaster. You wouldn't trust them after one good day — you'd check them three different ways. First: can they even tell rainy days from sunny ones? Second: when they say "70% chance," does it actually rain about 70% of those times, or are the numbers just for show? Third: on the days they scream "storm — cancel everything," is there really a storm often enough to be worth the panic? Three checks, because there are three completely different ways to be wrong, and a forecaster can sail through one while failing another.

The Big Picture

M-05 is a computer program that, the moment a patient arrives, predicts how many days they'll stay in the hospital — as an honest range, not a single blind guess.

Every hospital faces a quiet, constant question: when a patient checks in, how long will they stay? M-05 is a piece of software built to answer it — more precisely, it's a "model." A model here just means a computer program that has studied huge numbers of past patients, spotted the patterns in who stayed a long time and who left quickly, and uses those patterns to make an educated guess about a brand-new patient.

The name is nothing fancy. This project has a whole numbered line-up of these models — M-01, M-02, and so on — and M-05 is simply the one whose job is length of stay. The moment someone is admitted, M-05 predicts the number of days they're likely to be in the hospital.

That single answer unlocks a lot: when a bed will free up, how many nurses to schedule, when to book a follow-up. But M-05 does something smarter than blurt out one number. It hands back a range — like "about 4.2 days, likely between 2.8 and 6.1" — so staff can tell a rock-solid estimate apart from a shaky one. That honesty is the whole point.

It answers one question: how many days?
The moment a patient checks in, M-05 makes one guess: how many days will they stay in the hospital? That's the whole job — one clear question, one useful answer. Its answer can come out as a decimal, like 4.2 days, because it's a statistical estimate, not a room booking. 4.2 just means "a little over four days" (roughly four days and five hours) — the same way you'd say an average family has "2.3 kids" even though no one has a fifth of a child.
Why the hospital cares
Three big reasons. Beds — there are only so many, so knowing a patient needs about 5 days tells you when the next person can have that bed. Staffing — more people staying longer means more nurses to line up ahead of time. Follow-ups — you can book the after-visit checkup around the expected discharge day, before the patient has even unpacked.
A number, not a yes/no
Think of two kinds of questions. "Will it rain tomorrow?" has a yes-or-no answer — you're sorting the day into one of two buckets, meaning categories: rain or no-rain. "How many inches will it rain?" has a number answer instead. M-05 is the second kind. Predicting a number like this — rather than sorting things into categories — has a fancy name, regression, but that's all the word means: guess the number.
A range, not a single number
Here's the clever part. M-05 doesn't flatly say "4 days." It gives two things at once: a single best guess (about 4.2 days — its most likely answer, sitting in the middle) and a window around it (likely between 2.8 and 6.1). That window is called a confidence range, and "confidence" is an actual measured setting, not a mood. The range is built so that, when tested against thousands of real past patients, the true stay landed inside the window a set share of the time — say 9 times out of 10, which is a 90% range. That's what "checked" means: the window was proven honest against real past stays, not just guessed. Why bother? A bed coordinator needs to tell a solid "about 5 days" patient from a shaky "could be 3, could be 12" one. A narrow range means M-05 is sure; a wide range is its way of openly admitting "I'm not certain here." A single flat number hides that difference; the range shows it.
What it looks at
At admission, M-05 reads a handful of facts about the patient: age and sex; how they arrived (walk-in, by ambulance, or transferred from another hospital — someone rushed in by ambulance is often sicker, and tends to stay longer, than someone who strolls into a clinic); vital signs, which are the basic body readings nurses take first — temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, and breathing rate; past diseases they've had before; and early lab results, the first tests run on samples like blood or urine soon after arrival. Plus one special clue: the top diagnosis guesses from another model, M-01, which predicts what's probably medically wrong. M-01 doesn't hand over a single answer — it produces a ranked list of the most likely conditions, and M-05 takes the few at the top of that list. This matters a lot: someone likely dealing with pneumonia (a lung infection) stays very differently from someone with a broken arm. Feeding one model's answer into the next is called a cascade — picture water spilling down a fountain, one step at a time: M-01 fills its own pool first, then its result flows down into M-05.
How it turns all that into a number (it isn't magic)
So how does reading those facts become "4.2 days"? M-05 learned by example. Long before it ever saw a real patient, it was shown the records of a huge number of past patients — their age, vitals, diagnoses, and, crucially, how many days each one actually ended up staying. From all of that it worked out which combinations of facts tend to go with longer or shorter stays. When a new patient arrives, it matches their facts against those learned patterns and lands on a number. And if some of the facts are missing — say the labs aren't back yet because the patient only just walked in — M-05 still works: it leans on whatever it does have and simply widens its range to be honest about the extra uncertainty. As more information arrives, the guess sharpens.

Think of a hotel front desk. Every time a guest checks in, the manager quietly wonders, "How many nights will this one actually stay?" The answer tells them when the room frees up, how many staff to schedule, and when to prep for the next guest. A hospital has the exact same wish at every admission — and M-05 is the tool that answers it, for beds instead of rooms.

Step 2 of 6

What Goes In, What Comes Out

M-05 looks at everything we know by the time a patient is admitted, then hands back an honest range of days they'll likely stay — never a single fake-precise number.

Picture the hospital as a hotel that never turns anyone away. The front desk needs an early guess the moment a patient walks in: roughly how long will this person need a bed? "How long" means the number of days from being admitted to being discharged, counted on a continuous clock — so a quick in-and-out is a fraction of a day, and a single overnight is about one. That guess lets staff plan shifts, keep enough beds open, and line up follow-up appointments ahead of time. M-05 is that guess.

To make it, M-05 uses only what's known by the time staff are first sizing the patient up on arrival — their basic facts, how they got here, their first vital signs, their past health, and their earliest lab results. Nothing that reflects how the stay later unfolds is allowed in. (More on why that rule is so strict in a moment.)

And here's the part that surprises people: M-05 does not answer with one confident number like "5 days." Instead it gives a middle estimate wrapped in a likely range — an honest "about 4.2 days, probably somewhere between 2.8 and 6.1." A lone number sounds precise but is almost always a little wrong. A range is honest, and it's still plenty useful for planning.

The inputs: everything known when the patient is first sized up — and nothing from later
At admission, M-05 gathers the facts that already exist: age and sex; how the patient arrived (walked into the ER, scheduled surgery, transfer from another facility); the first snapshot of vital signs (blood pressure, heart rate, temperature); their past health history — meaning conditions that were formally diagnosed and recorded during earlier hospital stays in the system's records, not something a patient or a relative simply mentions in passing; and the earliest lab results from the arrival work-up. That's the whole intake picture: what's known about the patient before we see how their stay actually plays out.
One special input: a diagnosis forecast borrowed from another model (M-01)
M-05 also takes in the output of M-01, a separate tool whose only job is to predict what's likely wrong with the patient. M-01 doesn't hand over a vague hunch — it produces a ranked list of the most likely diagnoses, each tagged with a probability (a number between 0 and 1 saying how confident it is). M-05 feeds in the top few of these — the disease names paired with their scores — as extra inputs. Why bother? Because what's wrong with you strongly drives how long you stay: a suspected broken finger and suspected heart failure lead to very different stays. When one model's answer feeds straight into the next, that's called a cascade — like a relay race where the first runner hands the baton to the second. (M-01 and M-05 are just two members of a larger numbered family of models — M-02, M-03 and so on each do their own separate job. The numbers are only ID labels, not a ranking or an order of importance; only these two matter for this story.)
The output: not one number but three — a low, a middle, and a high
Guessing a number (rather than answering a yes/no question) is called regression. But M-05 doesn't stop at a single number — it's trained to output three at once: a low boundary, a middle estimate, and a high boundary. This is one model doing one pass; it simply learned during training to place three markers instead of one. (The technique is called quantile prediction: alongside the middle, the model learns where the 'bottom edge' and 'top edge' of the likely outcomes sit.) The gap between the low and high is the range — and it is not the same width for everyone. For a straightforward case the model is more sure, so it draws a tight range; for a complicated or unusual patient it's less sure, so it widens the range to be honest about that. A wide range is the model admitting 'this one's harder to call.'
What the '95%' actually promises — and how anyone knows it holds
The range is built to be right about 95% of the time: if you lined up 100 patients, roughly 95 of their real stays should fall inside the range M-05 drew for them. That isn't a hand-wave — it's checked. Before M-05 is trusted, the engineers run it on a large set of past patients whose real stay lengths are already known, then simply count how often the true stay landed inside the predicted range. If it comes out near 95%, good. If it's badly off — say only 80 out of 100 landed inside — the model is judged mis-calibrated and gets retuned (or held back) until it tells the truth about its own uncertainty. Why 95 and not 80 or 99? It's a dial the team chooses: aim higher and every range gets wider and less useful for planning; aim lower and the ranges get tight but miss the real answer too often. 95% is a long-standing convention that balances the two, which is why it's a common default in medicine and statistics.
Why the middle, not the average
The middle estimate is the median — the value smack in the center of all the plausible outcomes, with equal odds the real stay lands above or below it. And here's the group it's the middle OF: M-05 has learned from thousands of past patients, so for a new arrival it effectively pictures the whole spread of stays seen among patients who look like this one, and reports the center of that spread. Why the center rather than the average? Imagine a street of 19 houses worth $300k and one $10-million mansion: the average price gets yanked way up by that single mansion and stops describing any real house. Hospitals have the same quirk — a few rare, extreme stays (someone in the ICU for 100 days). The median ignores that distortion, so those rare cases don't drag every ordinary patient's estimate too high.
Why only admission-time information — the one unbreakable rule
M-05 is banned from using anything that reveals how the stay unfolds — later test results, treatments given, how the patient responds. But wait: don't even 'early' labs take an hour or two to come back, technically after the patient arrives? Yes — so the cutoff isn't the literal split-second of walking through the door. The line is drawn around the initial work-up: everything gathered while staff are first figuring out what's going on counts as intake, but anything that reflects the patient's response to treatment or how the stay progresses is off-limits. Two reasons for the rule. First, it's the only way to be useful — the whole point is to help staff plan before the stay unfolds, just as a weather forecast only helps if it comes before the rain. Second, it prevents accidental cheating: if information that only exists after the outcome sneaks into the training data, the model looks brilliant in testing but flops in real life, because that info simply isn't available when a real patient arrives. Data scientists call that trap leakage — like a student who's secretly seen the answer key: they ace the practice test, which tells you nothing about how they'll handle new questions.
Think of it like…
Think of a weather forecast. A good forecaster doesn't say "the high will be exactly 75 degrees" — that sounds precise but it's almost always a touch off. Instead they say "high around 75, likely between 70 and 80." That range is honest about the uncertainty, and it's still exactly what you need to decide whether to grab a jacket. M-05 talks the same way about days in a hospital bed: a sensible middle, wrapped in an honest range.

Why We Chose This Model

A flat "5 days" hides how sure we are, so we chose a model built to hand back an honest range instead of a single number.

M-05 — that's just our internal name for the model, like a version label — has one job: look at a patient who just arrived and guess how many days they'll stay. That number is gold for the hospital staff who plan beds, staffing, and follow-up appointments; we'll call that person the coordinator, and they often need the number before the patient has even settled into a room.

Notice this is a "how much" question, not a yes/no one. A yes/no question drops someone into a bucket (adult or not); a "how much" question wants a number that could land anywhere on a sliding scale — 1 day, 4 days, 12. Guessing a number like that is more like guessing someone's age than guessing whether they're an adult, and it has a name: regression — any model that predicts a number on a sliding scale, instead of picking a category, is doing regression.

But here's the idea that shaped every choice we made. Telling the coordinator "this patient will stay 5 days" sounds precise, yet it hides the one thing they most need to know: how sure are we? A rock-solid 5 and a shaky "could be anywhere from 2 to 12" call for completely different plans. So we didn't chase the best single number — we chose a model built to hand back an honest range.

One number is a trap
A single guess like "5 days" sounds confident but tells you nothing about how confident. Is it a sure thing, or could it swing wildly? Without that, the coordinator can't really plan. It's like a weather app that only says "it will rain" without telling you drizzle or downpour. So the real goal was never the single best guess — it was an honest range you can plan around.
Three models, one team (a 'quantile ensemble')
Instead of one model, M-05 trains three that work together: one learns the low end of the likely range, one the typical middle, one the high end. Together they draw a first-draft range like "about 4 days, likely between 3 and 6." A group of models teaming up is called an ensemble. The low/middle/high targets are quantiles — and a quantile is simply a cutoff point on the lineup of past patients: the value below which a set percentage of them fall (it's exactly the same thing as a percentile). Here the middle model aims for the 50th quantile (the halfway point), the low model for the 2.5th, and the high model for the 97.5th — so the low-to-high gap is built to span the middle 95% of likely outcomes. The clever part: all three see the exact same patient. What differs is each one's scoring rule — the rule that decides how badly a wrong guess gets punished (that "punishment" just IS the scoring rule doing its job). The low model is punished harshly for guessing too high, so it hugs the low side; the high model is punished harshly for guessing too low, so it leans high; the middle one is punished equally both ways. Same patient, three honest estimates. Hang onto that "first-draft" range — a later step checks and reshapes it.
What 'gradient-boosted trees' actually are
Each of the three models is built from gradient-boosted trees. A "tree" is just a branching checklist, like a triage nurse: "Temperature over 100.4? Yes, go this way. Heart rate over 100? Yes, go this way..." until it lands on a guess. Drawn out, it genuinely looks like an upside-down tree — a first question (the root), splits (branches), and final answers (leaves). "Boosting" means you don't stop at one checklist: you build one, spot where it's wrong, build a second whose only job is to fix those mistakes, then a third, and so on — hundreds of tiny correction-checklists stacked into one sharp answer. And "gradient" is how each new checklist figures out which way to correct: it measures the slope of the leftover errors — which direction the guesses are off, and how steeply — and steers the next checklist down that slope, the way you'd feel which way a hill drops and step downhill. That's all gradient-boosted trees means.
Two tricks that keep rare long stays from wrecking everything
Most patients go home in a few days, but a handful stay for weeks — and those rare cases can quietly drag every estimate too high. Fix one: before training, we compress stay-lengths onto a "squeezed" scale using a logarithm. A logarithm pulls big numbers inward far more than small ones — it turns 1, 10, 100, and 1,000 into the evenly spaced 0, 1, 2, 3 — so the yawning gap between a 90-day and a 100-day stay shrinks to almost nothing, while everyday 2-vs-5-day differences keep their full detail. The model learns in that shrunk world, then we run its answer back through the reverse of a logarithm (called exponentiating) to return to real days — so the coordinator only ever sees "4," never the scratch-work. Fix two: the middle guess uses the median — the middle value if you lined everyone up shortest to longest — instead of the average. The average gets dragged upward by a few 100-day patients (one giant number hauls the whole average toward it); the median barely budges, because it only cares who's standing in the middle of the line.
A calibration double-check you can trust
Remember that "first-draft" range the three models drew? Here's where it gets audited and turned into the final answer. A range is only useful if it's honest, so the system tests itself against real outcomes — and to keep the test fair, it holds back ("splits" off) a batch of past patients it never trained on, then checks how often the true stay actually landed inside the predicted range. Promised 95% but the truth only fell inside 88% of the time? It widens the range until the promise holds. Needlessly wide? It tightens it. That whole process is split-conformal calibration — "split" for the held-back data, "conformal" for the math that reshapes the range. The score it reports — "you said 95%, and the truth really landed inside about 95% of the time" — is called coverage. So calibration is the work; coverage is the grade. The three models propose a range; calibration is what turns it into a promise you can trust.
Why we passed on the simpler options
We seriously weighed plainer choices — a single boosted-tree model, plain straight-line linear regression, heavy deep-learning sequence models, and a classic event-counting method called Poisson. Poisson is built for counting how often something happens, and since a length of stay is really just a count of days, you can point it at this problem — but it quietly assumes the counts stay bunched close together, so the occasional 40- or 100-day stay violates that assumption and skews its estimates. And almost all of these options hand back just one number with no honest sense of uncertainty, dropping us right back into the "will it rain?" problem. For planning beds and staff, knowing the range is often worth more than the single best guess — so we chose the three-model approach precisely because it's built, from the ground up, to give a trustworthy range.
Think of it like…
Think of three weather forecasters looking at the same sky. One gives the optimistic forecast, one the most-likely, and one the gloomy one. Any single forecaster could be off — but put all three together and you get "probably sunny, but pack an umbrella just in case," which is far more useful for planning your day than one flat "it'll be nice."

Step 4 of 6

What Else Could We Use

Lots of models can guess one number for how many days a patient will stay — but only our three-model range tells you the guess AND how sure to be.

The job here is to guess how many days a patient will stay in the hospital. In machine-learning terms that's a "regression" problem — a fancy word that just means predicting a number (like "5 days"), not a yes/no answer. Plenty of tools can predict numbers. So before we settled on one, it's worth meeting the other candidates that were on the table.

Here's the thing that separates them all. Most of these tools hand you one number — "5 days" — and stop there. That's called a "point estimate": a single guess with no sense of how far off it might be. But a bed planner — the hospital staffer who decides which patient gets which bed and keeps the ward from running out of room — doesn't just need a guess. They need to know whether it's "definitely around 5" or "could be anywhere from 3 to 12."

So keep one question in mind as we meet the candidates: does this tool give an honest range, or just a lonely number?

Option 1: One boosted-tree, one number
A 'gradient-boosted tree' is a model built from many tiny yes/no rules of thumb. Each rule is a 'tree' — a short chain of questions like 'is age over 70? are the labs (the numbers from blood and other tests) out of normal range?' — and each new tree is added to fix the leftover mistakes of the ones before it. 'Gradient' just names the math trick it uses to see which direction those mistakes lean, so the next tree knows how to correct them. Think of a relay team where each runner patches the previous one's slip-ups. It's a strong guesser. But on its own it gives just one number, like a weather forecaster who says 'it'll be 72 degrees tomorrow' with no word on the chance of rain. Fine on average, but useless for knowing how confident to be.
Option 2: Plain linear regression
This is the simplest tool of all — imagine drawing one straight line through a cloud of scattered dots and reading your answer off it. It assumes everything adds up in neat, straight-line steps ('each extra year of age adds exactly 0.1 days'). But real stays are lumpy: one bad diagnosis can add ten days at once, not a tenth of one. The straight line is just too crude to catch that.
Option 3: Deep-learning time models (TPC, LSTM)
These are the heavy machinery — types of neural network (models loosely inspired by how brain cells connect) built to read data that arrives over time. The names are just labels for two such designs: LSTM stands for 'Long Short-Term Memory,' meaning it holds on to earlier readings while taking in new ones; TPC stands for 'Temporal Pointwise Convolution,' another way of scanning a patient's readings across time. Picture a super-smart assistant reading a live, minute-by-minute diary of the patient and updating the estimate constantly. Genuinely powerful — but they need a steady stream of data and lots of computing muscle to learn well. Here we only get one snapshot, taken the moment the patient walks in. Feeding a single snapshot to a machine built for a live feed is overkill.
Option 4: Poisson models
Poisson is a statistics tool built for counting rare events that happen at a steady average rate — like how many calls a call center gets in an hour. Its 'counting pattern' is the assumption that events pop up independently and evenly, which produces a fairly tight, predictable spread of counts. Hospital stays break that assumption. They're 'skewed' — lopsided, with most stays bunched down at the short end (a few days) — and they have a 'long tail': a small number of patients who stay a very, very long time, stretching far out to the right. That lopsided shape with rare extreme values looks nothing like Poisson's neat, even pattern, so the fit is awkward from the very start.
The one thing they all share
Notice the pattern: nearly every option hands back a single point estimate — one number, with no honest range around it. It sounds confident. But confidence without a range is exactly what leaves a bed planner unprepared when the real stay lands nowhere near the guess.
Why the three-model range wins
The chosen design uses the very same gradient-boosted trees from Option 1 — but instead of training one, it trains three and reads them as a team. (A group of models working together like this is called an 'ensemble.') So the winner isn't a fancier tool; it's the same tool used three times, each pointed at a different part of the range. Here's the trick that makes one aim low and another aim high: during training you can change how a model is scored. Punish it harder for guessing too high than too low, and it learns to land low; flip the punishment and it aims high. The exact spots it aims for are called 'quantiles' — a quantile is simply the mark below which a set share of cases fall (on a test, the mark where only 10% of scores sit below you is a quantile). We aim the low model at the 2.5% mark and the high model at the 97.5% mark, so the gap between them is meant to cover the middle 95% of patients — that's where the '95% range' comes from. The middle model aims for the exact center. The three train as separate runs — they don't whisper to each other during training — and we simply read their three answers side by side.
What makes the range trustworthy
Two more touches keep the range honest. First, the middle model targets the median rather than the average. The median is the calculated middle value — line every patient up from shortest to longest stay and it's the length at the halfway point (a computed figure, not necessarily any one real patient's exact stay). Using it means a handful of 100-day patients can't drag the typical estimate upward the way an average would. Second, a 'calibration' step checks the range holds up. Calibration just means testing whether a stated confidence matches reality: we take past patients whose real stay we already know, count how often the true length actually landed inside each predicted 95% range, and confirm it happens about 95 times out of 100. If it doesn't, we adjust the models until it does.
Think of it like…
Think of a car mechanic. One says, "The repair will be $500." Another says, "$400 to $650, most likely around $500." The first sounds confident but might leave you short. The second tells you what to actually budget for. The alternative models are the first mechanic — a single, lonely number. The chosen three-model approach is the second: a best guess, plus the honest range you can plan around.

Step 5 of 6

How We Know It Works

We grade M-05 with three checks: how close its best guess is, how much it truly explains about each patient, and whether its "95% range" can actually be trusted.

When a bed coordinator asks M-05 "how long will this patient stay?", it doesn't blurt out a single number. M-05 is a computer model — a piece of software that learned patterns from hundreds of thousands of past hospital stays — and it answers like a careful person would: "probably about 4 days, but realistically somewhere between 3 and 6." That's two answers bundled into one: a best guess, and a safety range around it.

So how do we know if M-05 is any good? We test it on real patients from the past — people already discharged, so we know exactly how long they actually stayed. We let M-05 make its prediction using only what was known the day they arrived, then compare that prediction to what really happened. Because the answer has two halves, we grade both — the guess and the range — separately, using three yardsticks. (A yardstick is just a measuring stick: a simple way to score how well something did.)

And here's why all three matter together: a model can look great on average while its ranges quietly lie about how sure it really is. Checking all three is what keeps M-05 honest.

One model, three numbers (not a committee)
M-05 gives three numbers — a low, a middle, and a high — that together draw the range. But that's not three separate models arguing. It's one model, like a single experienced nurse who has seen thousands of patients. Ask her about a new one and she says: "The typical patient like this stayed about 4 days, but some stayed as few as 3 and some as many as 6." Same brain, same knowledge of the patient — she just reports the middle, the floor, and the ceiling of what happened to similar people before. Under the hood the model isn't guessing three times at random; it runs the same patient through the same learned knowledge with the dial set three ways — once tuned to land on the typical middle, once to find the low edge, once the high edge — three related calculations sharing one brain. It sets the low number where only about 2 or 3 out of 100 similar patients stayed shorter, and the high number where only about 2 or 3 stayed longer. That leaves about 95 out of 100 landing inside — and that's what makes it a "95% range."
MAE — how far off is the best guess?
MAE stands for Mean Absolute Error. Ignore the fancy name — it just means: for every patient, see how many days the guess missed by (too high or too low doesn't matter), then average it. It's like a weather app that says "75 degrees today" — MAE is simply "on average, how many degrees off was it?" An MAE of 1.2 means M-05's day-guess is off by about 1.2 days on average. Lower is better. It tells a coordinator how much to trust that quick number day-to-day.
R-squared — is it really learning about each patient?
Some patients stay 1 day, others stay 20 — there's a lot of natural variety in stay length. R-squared asks: of all that variety, how much can the model actually explain from what it knows (age; vitals — basic body readings like temperature, heart rate, and blood pressure; and the likely diagnosis), versus how much stays a mystery? Don't worry about the name — like MAE, "R-squared" comes from statistics (it involves squaring some numbers), but all you need to know is that it's a score from 0 to 1. A 0 means the model is no better than lazily guessing "the average stay" for everyone. A 1 means it explains every difference perfectly. Think of guessing how long a phone call will last: knowing nothing, you'd guess the average every time; knowing who's calling and why, you'd guess far better. R-squared measures how much that patient-specific info actually helps.
So what R-squared is "good"? Lower than you'd expect
Here's the catch: there's no universal passing grade — it depends on how naturally messy the thing is to predict. Guessing adult height from parents' height might score around 0.8 (that's a rough, illustrative figure, not an official benchmark) because height is mostly genetics. Hospital stay is the opposite: a huge chunk depends on things invisible at admission — whether a specialist is free on Monday, whether family is ready to receive the patient at home, whether an insurance approval stalls. No model can see those. So for stay length, a rough rule of thumb: 0.2 to 0.3 is a real but partial signal; 0.4 to 0.5 is genuinely good; near 0 is a red flag. And oddly, a score above 0.5 is worth a second look too — not to celebrate, but to make sure nothing "leaked." Leakage is when the model accidentally got a peek at information it wouldn't really have on admission day — usually a clue that only exists because the stay already happened (say, a discharge note, or a treatment that only gets ordered for long stays). That makes the score look brilliant in testing but useless in real life, because on a brand-new patient that clue doesn't exist yet. So a surprisingly high score is a signal to double-check we didn't accidentally let the future sneak in. A clean 0.5 is something to celebrate, not apologize for.
Coverage — is the safety range honest?
This checks the second half of the answer: the range. M-05 is built to give a 95% range, meaning the true stay should fall inside it 95 times out of 100. Coverage is us going back and checking whether it actually did. When a model's stated confidence matches how often it's really right, we call it well-calibrated — a plain way of saying "it means what it says." Picture a forecaster who each morning gives a range for the day's high temperature and claims the real high will land inside it 95% of the time. Over 100 days, if the real high actually landed in their range about 95 times, they're well-calibrated and you can trust them. If M-05 says "95% range: 3 to 6 days" but the truth lands there only 70% of the time, the range isn't honest — and a coordinator who trusts it gets blindsided.
Why 95%, not 100% — and why a huge range can't cheat
Why not just demand 100%? Because "always right" and "actually useful" pull in opposite directions. A model could say "somewhere between 1 and 30 days" for everyone and be right 99% of the time — and be completely useless for planning, like a forecaster saying "temperature between freezing and boiling." Wider range = easier to be right but tells you less; narrower = more useful but riskier. 95% is the deliberate compromise: we accept that roughly 1 in 20 patients falls outside the range, in exchange for a range tight enough to schedule beds with. That's exactly why coverage is never judged alone — we also check width, sometimes called sharpness. (A tight range points to one clear answer, like a sharp pencil; a wide range is blurry and vague — hence "sharp.") Are the ranges a few days wide, not a lazy "1 to 30"? The real prize is a model that hits 95% AND stays tight — confident and honest at the same time.
Why three yardsticks, and why we use the median
One number alone can fool you: a great MAE can hide unreliable ranges. So we use all three together — MAE checks the middle guess is accurate, R-squared checks the model uses patient-specific info instead of parroting the average, and coverage (plus width) checks the range is trustworthy. One last detail: the "best guess" is the median, not a plain average. The median is the true middle — line up all the similar real patients we recorded, shortest stay to longest, and the median is the one standing in the exact middle of that line. Why not a plain average? Because a few patients staying 100-plus days would drag a plain average way up, making "typical" look longer than it really is. The median ignores those rare extremes and reflects what a normal patient actually experiences, which is exactly what bed planning needs.
Think of it like…
Think of a trustworthy weather forecaster. Each morning they say "the high will be about 75, and I'm 95% sure it'll land between 72 and 78." To decide if you can trust them, you check two things. Were their best-guess temperatures usually close to reality? (That's MAE.) And when they claimed a "95% range," did the real high actually fall inside it about 95 days out of 100? (That's coverage.) A forecaster who hedges with "somewhere between freezing and boiling" is technically never wrong but totally useless — which is why we also check that their ranges stay tight. M-05 earns your trust exactly the same way.
